# MYTHROOT PREHISTORIC / ANACHRONISTIC SURVIVAL FANTASY WORLD PROFILE
## Specialized Worldbuilding Profile — v1

**Status:** Authoritative  
**Profile Type:** Prehistoric / Anachronistic Survival Fantasy

# 1. Purpose

This profile supplements the Mythroot World Standard. It defines the specialized worldbuilding requirements for settings in which human societies exist inside a powerful prehistoric or prehistoric-inspired ecosystem, including settings that deliberately combine species, environments, or conditions that did not historically coexist.

The profile is not a prehistoric encyclopedia and does not require scientific realism as its final goal.

Its defining principle is:

> **Human civilization is an ecological participant rather than an ecological master.**

The profile exists to prevent the world from becoming:

- a dinosaur theme park;
- generic fantasy with prehistoric creatures pasted onto it;
- modern people wearing prehistoric technology;
- a collection of scientifically accurate facts with no lived culture;
- or a survival setting in which ecology is only scenery.

The profile defines what must be understood, not what must be exhaustively documented.

# 2. Prehistoric / Anachronistic Survival Fantasy Identity

**Development Status:** Mature  
**Importance:** CORE

## Core Definition

A world where human societies must build meaningful lives inside an ecosystem that remains larger, older, and more powerful than civilization.

The world may be scientifically grounded, heavily fantastical, or deliberately impossible. The requirement is not historical accuracy.

The requirement is **internal ecological and social causality**.

## Core World Premise

What makes the world prehistoric, prehistoric-inspired, or anachronistic?

If species from different geological periods coexist, what makes their coexistence feel deliberate rather than arbitrary?

> **Anachronism may be impossible; it must not be arbitrary.**

## Core Creative Promise

The world should make:

- environmental knowledge feel powerful;
- cooperation feel necessary;
- animals feel like living participants;
- geography feel consequential;
- resources matter;
- injury matter;
- cultural knowledge matter;
- survival feel earned.

## Intended Experience

Define the dominant two or three experiences the world is meant to produce.

Possible dimensions include:

- awe;
- wonder;
- danger;
- vulnerability;
- discovery;
- belonging;
- intimacy;
- brutality;
- mystery;
- transformation;
- serenity;
- reverence;
- isolation;
- resilience;
- interdependence.

Two worlds can share the same ecological premise while producing radically different experiences.

A world where humanity is constantly threatened and a world where humanity has spent thousands of years learning to live beautifully alongside megafauna may use the same basic premise while being fundamentally different worlds.

# 3. Priority Reallocation

**Importance:** CORE

This is the section that most distinguishes this profile from a generic fantasy, survival, or historical world.

## What Becomes More Important

- ecology and food webs;
- climate, water, and seasonality;
- migration and animal behavior;
- predator/prey relationships;
- human subsistence;
- mobility;
- resource distribution and consequence;
- injury, medicine, recovery, and role adaptation;
- environmental knowledge and cultural memory;
- material technology;
- cooperation and population pressure;
- cultural adaptation;
- daily life.

## What Becomes Less Important

- elaborate bureaucracies unless the world specifically develops them;
- industrial infrastructure;
- mass standing armies;
- highly centralized governments;
- complex financial systems;
- extensive urbanization;
- abstract political structures without material causes;
- technology unrelated to available materials, energy, labor, and knowledge.

This does not mean such things cannot exist.

It means they must earn their place.

## What Can Stay Light

The following may remain intentionally open when they do not affect the intended world:

- distant regions;
- minor species;
- obscure history without present consequences;
- exact evolutionary ancestry where the setting deliberately departs from Earth history;
- distant cultures;
- minor technologies;
- cosmological explanations that do not affect current world logic.

Open space is encouraged when its boundaries are intentional.

# 4. Ecological Architecture

**Importance:** CORE

The ecosystem must be understandable as a system even when its history is fantastical.

Define enough of the major relationships between:

- climate;
- vegetation;
- herbivores;
- predators;
- scavengers;
- decomposers;
- humans.

## Ecological Niche

Major species should have differentiated ecological roles.

Ask:

> **What does this species do in the ecosystem besides look impressive?**

If many large species coexist, establish how they partition:

- food;
- habitat;
- season;
- movement;
- vegetation layer;
- prey;
- territory;
- or another meaningful resource.

## Ecological Consequence

Large animals should change the world around them.

Ask:

- What vegetation do they alter?
- What trails or migration corridors do they create?
- What predators follow them?
- What resources do they make available?
- What damage or disturbance do they cause?
- What happens if they disappear?

> **Large creatures should have large consequences.**

# 5. Human Ecological Embeddedness

**Importance:** CORE

Humans should be designed as participants in the ecosystem rather than as a separate category of worldbuilding.

Define:

- what people eat;
- where resources come from;
- how reliable those resources are;
- why people move;
- why they stay;
- what makes them vulnerable;
- what environmental knowledge they possess;
- what they cannot safely control.

## Human Vulnerability

Human intelligence must not become a universal excuse for ignoring physical differences.

Humans may compensate through:

- preparation;
- numbers;
- tools;
- terrain;
- traps;
- knowledge;
- coordination;
- technology;
- magic.

Those advantages should still have limitations and costs.

## Human Adaptation

Ask:

> **What has humanity learned because this ecosystem forced it to learn?**

The answer should influence:

- technology;
- social organization;
- education;
- culture;
- settlement;
- religion;
- economy;
- identity.

# 6. Subsistence & Resource Systems

**Importance:** CORE

Define only the level of resource detail necessary to understand how society actually survives.

Consider:

- hunting;
- gathering;
- fishing;
- trapping;
- scavenging;
- cultivation, where appropriate;
- animal management, where appropriate;
- food storage;
- preservation;
- water;
- seasonal abundance and scarcity.

## Resource Consequence

A successful or failed resource event should have consequences beyond the immediate scene.

Ask:

> **What changes if this resource becomes scarce?**

and:

> **What changes if it becomes abundant?**

Resource systems should influence:

- population;
- movement;
- conflict;
- trade;
- social status;
- labor;
- technology;
- cultural practice.

# 7. Mobility & Settlement

**Importance:** CORE

Define:

- why people move;
- when they move;
- what makes them stay;
- what movement costs;
- what movement makes possible;
- what resources justify permanent settlement;
- what forces relocation.

## Settlement Test

Ask:

> **Why is this settlement here instead of somewhere else?**

Then ask:

> **What would force everyone here to leave?**

Settlements should respond to:

- water;
- food;
- migration;
- terrain;
- predators;
- climate;
- storage;
- trade;
- defensibility;
- cultural history.

# 8. Megafauna & Animal Agency

**Importance:** CORE

Important animals should have lives independent of human stories.

Define, where relevant:

- ecological role;
- habitat;
- diet;
- social structure;
- movement;
- reproduction;
- defense;
- predators;
- competitors;
- seasonal behavior;
- human relationship;
- cultural significance;
- material/resource value.

## Animal Agency Test

Ask:

> **Would this animal still behave this way if no human character were present?**

A predator is not evil because it hunts humans.

A herbivore is not harmless because it eats plants.

An animal may become dangerous because of:

- territorial defense;
- parental behavior;
- mating;
- fear;
- food competition;
- migration;
- accidental proximity.

Do not turn every creature into a monster.

# 9. Human Technology

**Importance:** CORE

Technology must arise from:

- available materials;
- energy;
- labor;
- knowledge;
- time;
- environmental constraints.

The absence of industrial technology is not evidence of intellectual inferiority.

## Technology Pressure

Ask:

> **What prevents the next technological step?**

The answer should arise from the world.

Also ask:

> **What problem does this technology solve that this environment actually creates?**

# 10. Injury, Medicine & Care

**Importance:** CORE

A survival society must have systems for people who cannot temporarily or permanently perform their previous roles.

Consider:

- wounds;
- fractures;
- infection;
- disease;
- childbirth;
- pain;
- plant medicines;
- surgery;
- spiritual healing;
- rehabilitation;
- adaptive roles;
- social care.

## Injury Test

Ask:

> **What happens to a person after the dramatic moment is over?**

Injury should have social and economic consequences, but permanent impairment should not automatically mean social uselessness.

# 11. Culture & Daily Life

**Importance:** CORE

Culture should emerge partly from:

- environment;
- food;
- danger;
- mobility;
- climate;
- resources;
- technology;
- population;
- history.

Define enough to make ordinary life inhabitable:

- food preparation;
- clothing;
- hygiene;
- childcare;
- education;
- work;
- tool repair;
- entertainment;
- storytelling;
- seasonal routines;
- animal knowledge.

## Cultural Adaptation Test

Ask:

> **Would this custom still exist if the environment were radically different?**

If not, the environmental cause should be understood.

> **Ecology should create culture.**

# 12. Community & Cooperation

**Importance:** CORE

Cooperation should solve actual survival problems rather than exist only as a moral ideal.

Consider:

- food sharing;
- labor;
- child-rearing;
- care for injured people;
- elder knowledge;
- hunting groups;
- seasonal cooperation;
- conflict resolution;
- knowledge transmission.

## Knowledge as a Resource

Ask:

> **What knowledge does the community depend on, and what happens if the person who knows it dies?**

Environmental knowledge can function as:

- wealth;
- status;
- inheritance;
- trade;
- political power;
- cultural identity.

# 13. Politics & Power

**Importance:** SUPPORTING / CORE where resource control produces formal power

Politics should emerge from actual survival relationships.

Ask:

- Who controls food?
- Who controls water?
- Who controls knowledge?
- Who controls movement?
- Who controls dangerous territory?
- What creates authority?
- What makes authority legitimate?
- What happens when authority becomes harmful?

Do not assume either centralized states or egalitarian societies.

Both require causes.

# 14. Economy & Exchange

**Importance:** SUPPORTING / CORE where resource exchange materially shapes society

Define:

- what has value;
- what is scarce;
- what is abundant;
- what can be stored;
- what can be transported;
- what must be consumed quickly;
- what knowledge has economic value;
- what materials create trade networks.

Avoid importing later economic structures simply because they are familiar.

# 15. Religion, Belief & Meaning-Making

**Importance:** SUPPORTING / CORE where belief materially shapes adaptation

Consider how cultures interpret:

- animals;
- death;
- weather;
- migration;
- illness;
- dreams;
- ancestors;
- unusual phenomena;
- ecological disasters.

Do not assume every prehistoric culture is automatically animistic.

Belief should emerge from the specific world.

# 16. Magic / Supernatural Systems

**Importance:** SUPPORTING / CORE where present

If magic exists, define:

- source;
- access;
- limitations;
- costs;
- reliability;
- consequences;
- relationship to ecology;
- relationship to animals;
- relationship to injury;
- relationship to death;
- social consequences.

## Magic Consequence Rule

> **Magic may reduce survival difficulty without erasing survival consequence.**

If magic does erase a major consequence, that should be a deliberate foundational choice rather than an accidental convenience.

# 17. Prehistoric / Anachronistic Canon Management

Use the standard Mythroot canon categories:

### HARD CANON

Established ecological, historical, technological, cultural, or supernatural facts.

### FLEXIBLE CANON

Established, but adaptable within defined boundaries.

### OPEN

Not established and safe for future invention.

### UNKNOWN

Known to exist or matter, but deliberately unexplained.

### IN-WORLD BELIEF

What inhabitants believe about the world, whether or not it is true.

For anachronistic premises, also distinguish:

### ESTABLISHED FUNCTION

What a species, phenomenon, technology, or supernatural force is established to do.

### IN-WORLD UNDERSTANDING

What people believe about it or understand about its operation.

These should not automatically be identical.

# 18. Prehistoric / Anachronistic Story Engines

**Importance:** CORE

Identify repeatable engines such as:

- dangerous hunts;
- migration stories;
- ecological change;
- predator expansion;
- resource scarcity;
- resource abundance;
- discovery of unknown territory;
- loss or recovery of cultural knowledge;
- apprenticeship;
- inter-community resource conflict;
- settlement versus mobility;
- human-animal relationships;
- ecological catastrophe;
- cultural change;
- survival of injured or displaced people;
- conflicts between competing survival philosophies.

The setting should generate stories at multiple scales:

- individual;
- family;
- community;
- regional;
- ecological;
- generational.

# 19. Character Compatibility

**Importance:** SUPPORTING

The world naturally supports:

- hunters;
- gatherers;
- trackers;
- healers;
- toolmakers;
- environmental specialists;
- storytellers;
- elders and knowledge keepers;
- scouts;
- diplomats;
- traders;
- displaced people;
- settlement founders;
- cultural reformers;
- people questioning established survival practices.

Do not make these mandatory archetypes.

They are examples of roles naturally generated by the world.

# 20. Prehistoric / Anachronistic Coherence Audit

Check:

- [ ] Major environments support the species and resources placed within them.
- [ ] Anachronistic species coexist through deliberate internal logic.
- [ ] Major animals have ecological roles beyond spectacle.
- [ ] Human population, settlement, mobility, and subsistence are compatible with resources.
- [ ] Human intelligence does not erase physical constraints.
- [ ] Technology arises from available materials, knowledge, labor, and environmental pressure.
- [ ] Important animals have behavior independent of human characters.
- [ ] Major ecological changes create downstream consequences.
- [ ] Culture contains practices that make sense for the environment and history.
- [ ] Injury, disability, and care have meaningful social consequences.
- [ ] Magic does not accidentally erase the world's defining pressures.
- [ ] Intended experience is actually produced by the world's systems.
- [ ] The world does not require exhaustive documentation of every species or minor detail.
- [ ] Open areas remain intentionally open rather than arbitrary.

# 21. Prehistoric / Anachronistic World Usability Test

An outside creator should be able to answer:

1. What makes this world prehistoric or anachronistic?
2. What is the dominant intended experience?
3. What ecological systems must remain coherent?
4. Why can the major species coexist?
5. How do humans actually obtain food and water?
6. Why do communities move or settle?
7. What do the major animals do when humans are not present?
8. What technologies exist and what pressures produced them?
9. How does injury affect individuals and communities?
10. What cultures have emerged from the environment?
11. What knowledge is valuable?
12. What can the creator invent freely?
13. What must remain consistent?
14. What kinds of stories naturally work here?

# 22. Prehistoric / Anachronistic Story Generation Test

The setting should support, at minimum:

- [ ] A dangerous hunt.
- [ ] A migration or seasonal movement story.
- [ ] An ecological-change story.
- [ ] A community/resource conflict.
- [ ] An intimate family or relationship story.
- [ ] A knowledge-loss or apprenticeship story.
- [ ] A discovery story.
- [ ] A story involving permanent or temporary injury.
- [ ] A story in which an animal is dangerous without being an antagonist.
- [ ] A story in which the environment changes the outcome more than any individual character does.

# 23. Common Failure Modes

## Dinosaur Theme Park

Cool prehistoric creatures exist, but their presence does not meaningfully alter the world.

## Caveman Stereotype

Limited technology is treated as limited intelligence or culture.

## Modern People in Stone Tools

Characters retain contemporary assumptions without showing the consequences of living inside a fundamentally different resource system.

## Random Anachronism

Species from unrelated eras coexist because they look cool, without an internal ecological or speculative explanation.

## Human Apex Fantasy

Human intelligence becomes a universal excuse for ignoring physical differences and environmental constraints.

## Creature-as-Monster

Every dangerous animal behaves like an antagonist whose only purpose is to threaten protagonists.

## Hunt-as-Action-Scene

The hunt ends when the animal dies, ignoring preparation, injury, processing, distribution, preservation, labor loss, and future consequences.

## Magic-as-Undo Button

Magic quietly eliminates scarcity, injury, mortality, or ecological consequence whenever convenient.

## Universal Prehistoric Culture

Every human population behaves the same despite different environments, resources, histories, and pressures.

## Generic Fantasy Institutions

Kingdoms, guilds, armies, cities, taverns, and other familiar structures appear without material or historical causes.

## Agriculture by Assumption

Later crops, livestock, dairy, textiles, preservation methods, or food systems appear without being supported by the world's actual flora, fauna, technology, and history.

## Ecological Wallpaper

The world looks prehistoric but its ecology never changes what people do.

# 25. Final Prehistoric / Anachronistic Survival Fantasy Test

Ask:

> **Does humanity feel like it belongs to an ecosystem rather than standing above it?**

Then:

> **If I remove the prehistoric/anachronistic ecosystem, does the culture still make sense?**

Then:

> **If I restore the ecosystem, does it actively change the way people live, think, organize, move, work, believe, and tell stories?**

Finally:

> **Could another creator add a creature, settlement, culture, or story without breaking the world's ecological and social logic?**

If yes, the profile is doing its job.

# 26. Core Design Principle

> **Human civilization is an ecological participant rather than an ecological master.**

The goal is not to recreate prehistory.

The goal is to create a world in which the environment is powerful enough, coherent enough, and consequential enough that humanity's relationship with it becomes one of the world's primary sources of culture, conflict, beauty, danger, and story.

# 28. Research Notes

This section is supporting material for Mythroot's internal development record and is not required creator reading.

The profile was developed using the Mythroot Profile Creation methodology: subject grounding, practitioner/craft study, representative works, community practice, and legitimate open/non-licensed resources. Research was distilled into FACT → TECHNIQUE → PRINCIPLE → PROFILE DECISION.

Key research findings included:

- Real Pleistocene ecosystems demonstrate that non-analog ecological communities can contain many large species through differentiated niches.
- Large herbivores can alter vegetation, nutrient availability, smaller-animal abundance, and ecosystem structure.
- Human mobility and cooperation can function as adaptations to unpredictable resources.
- Paleopathological evidence demonstrates that severe injury can coexist with survival through social care.
- Prehistoric animal life becomes more immersive when behavior, habitat, reproduction, social structure, and ecological relationships matter.
- Successful speculative settings often connect creature/environment behavior to human culture rather than treating creatures as decorative content.
- Prehistoric tabletop settings demonstrate the importance of turning setting premise into creator-usable structures.
- Open biodiversity and paleontological datasets can support legitimate research without treating another creator's expression as a template.

The research archive may be expanded as the world is built. The profile itself should remain the distilled creator-facing methodology.

# 29. Versioning

**Mythroot Prehistoric / Anachronistic Survival Fantasy World Profile v1**

This is the authoritative v1 profile.

After the first world is built using this profile, review:

- missing questions;
- unnecessary sections;
- areas requiring more or less depth;
- whether Intended Experience improves differentiation;
- whether the profile remains useful for both harsh survival worlds and mature ecological civilizations;
- whether the classification should continue to use “Survival Fantasy” or be broadened toward “Human Ecological Embeddedness”;
- creator usability;
- new research-derived principles.

The goal is not to create the perfect prehistoric checklist.

The goal is to create a profile that **makes better prehistoric/anachronistic worlds.**
