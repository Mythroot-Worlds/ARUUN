## 0. CANON RULE FOR HEARTH

This document exists to prevent the project from requiring a retrospective review of hundreds of source documents every time canon needs to be identified.

### Default canon rule

Material that has been deliberately placed in the Hearth project and documented as a decision is treated as **canon by default**.

A statement is **not canon only when the source document explicitly marks it as**:
- questionable;
- rejected;
- superseded;
- non-canon;
- an unresolved alternative that has not been selected;
- or otherwise explicitly provisional in a way that says it is not yet part of the setting.

Words such as **working**, **draft**, or **update** do not by themselves invalidate a documented decision. If a document contains a clearly stated setting decision and does not explicitly reject that decision, the decision is retained here.

Where two Hearth documents genuinely conflict, this consolidation preserves the most recently established decision where the source clearly indicates that one supersedes the other. If no supersession can be established, the contradiction is recorded for resolution rather than silently rewritten.

### Important distinction

**Canon does not mean immutable.** It means this is the current established state of Hearth. Future decisions may revise it deliberately.

# PART I — HEARTH AS A WORLD

## 1. Continental Identity

Hearth is a mostly intact great continental landmass. Its defining geographic character is continuity: enormous interior distances, broad connected watersheds, old continental terrain, and mountain systems concentrated toward portions of its active margins.

Major structures include:
- broad ancient continental interior;
- major mountain systems near active margins;
- older, heavily eroded interior uplands;
- large river basins originating in mountain regions;
- broad floodplains and wetlands;
- coastal plains and estuaries;
- small peripheral island systems;
- interior basins and plateaus;
- localized rain-shadow regions behind major ranges.

Hearth's ecological identity is **connected abundance**: large continuous habitats and long terrestrial migration routes.

## 2. Climate

### Coastal
Ocean-moderated. Depending on currents and wind direction, coastal Hearth contains humid forests, dense low vegetation, coastal grasslands, wetlands, and storm-exposed regions.

### Mountain-facing
Wet windward slopes, forests, wet uplands, and major headwaters.

### Leeward
Rain shadows produce dry woodland, grassland, scrub, semi-arid basins, and localized arid regions.

### Central interior
Strong continental seasonality produces enormous seasonal grasslands, woodland mosaics, broad river plains, dry forests, and interior basins.

### Cooler regions
Seasonal forests, cool grasslands, uplands, and cold-adapted ecosystems occur at higher elevations and latitudes.

## 3. Hydrology

Mountains function as major watersheds:

**mountains → precipitation → headwaters → rivers → floodplains/wetlands → coastal deltas**

Hearth's major drainage architecture includes:

- **Great Western/Southwestern Watershed:** Frostward/Aegir → Stonehearth → Great Plains → Greenmarsh/SilverSpine → coast.
- **Great Plains:** broad drainage mosaic rather than a single river basin.
- **Greenmarsh:** low-gradient receiving basin with wetlands, lakes, backwaters, and distributaries.
- **Northern Chain:** northern uplands → lake systems → rivers → Greenmarsh and/or northern coast.
- **Sunscour:** major closed/endorheic basin draining inward to an inland lake/salt system.
- **Southward Range:** major watershed divide separating interior and southern coastal drainage.
- **Embercoast:** short, steep volcanic rivers draining to coastal plains/estuaries.
- **Eastern/southeastern uplands:** shorter direct-to-coast drainage systems.

The **SilverSpine Delta** is the terminal deltaic complex of the principal continental river network.

Exact minor channels, springs, tributaries, flood extents, and local river names remain open for regional mapping where not otherwise established.

## 4. Major Geographic Regions

The established Hearth geographic layer includes:

- Great Plains
- SilverSpine Delta
- Greenmarsh Lowlands
- Northern Wilds
- Stonehearth Uplands
- Sunscour Basin
- Frostward Range
- Aegir Mountains
- Southward Range
- Embercoast Volcanic Arc
- Stormcoast Peninsula
- Emerald Gulf
- Dawnpoint Peninsula
- Coastal Isles / Isle Chains

These geographic regions form the environmental foundation from which human geography follows.

## 5. Human Geography Principle

Hearth human geography follows:

**environment → water → resources → human corridors → population density → contact/isolation → peoples**

It does not begin with arbitrary settlements and build the environment around them.

# PART II — HEARTH HUMAN POPULATION

## 6. Continental Population

Hearth's established working human population is approximately **1.5 million**.

Continental allocation:

| Human zone | Share | Approx. population |
|---|---:|---:|
| River valleys & floodplains | 25% | 375,000 |
| Grassland / woodland mosaic | 30% | 450,000 |
| Wetlands & major lake systems | 15% | 225,000 |
| Temperate/subtropical coasts | 12% | 180,000 |
| Highland valleys | 8% | 120,000 |
| Dry interior / rain shadows | 7% | 105,000 |
| Other scattered / seasonal | 3% | 45,000 |
| **TOTAL** | **100%** | **1,500,000** |

These are continental allocation categories rather than literal geographic borders.

Typical residential communities are approximately **80–150 people**, with smaller groups and larger seasonal aggregations also possible.

## 7. Density Categories

Hearth population mapping uses five broad density categories:

- **D1 — HIGH:** repeatedly productive environments capable of supporting persistent communities and large seasonal aggregations.
- **D2 — MODERATE:** reliable resources with greater seasonal or geographic constraints.
- **D3 — LOW:** small populations concentrated around specific resources.
- **D4 — SEASONAL:** temporary occupation or aggregation rather than continuous residential presence.
- **D5 — TRANSIT / NEAR-EMPTY:** movement corridors, dangerous terrain, extreme environments, or areas lacking sufficient reliable resources for sustained occupation.

Density describes population concentration, not cultural importance.

## 8. Human Contact / Isolation

High-contact zones include:
- major river junctions;
- delta/estuary systems;
- broad grassland corridors;
- accessible coastal resource zones;
- mountain passes.

Intermediate-contact zones include:
- woodland margins;
- foothills;
- lake chains;
- mixed-resource transition zones.

Low-contact zones include:
- isolated mountain valleys;
- dry interior water pockets;
- difficult volcanic terrain;
- isolated islands;
- extreme environments.

# PART III — THE SIX HEARTH PEOPLES / CULTURAL SYSTEMS

## 9. PLAINS

### Core social character
Plains society operates through overlapping layers:
1. household/family life;
2. mobile work and travel groups;
3. recurring anchor communities;
4. the broader Plains cultural network.

Mobile groups are generally around 10–25 people, with 10–15 common. They are flexible rather than permanently familial.

Anchor communities provide recurring places for elders who can no longer travel, very young children, injured people, craft workers, teachers, and stored resources.

### Mobility
Plains people are highly mobile to semi-mobile. Movement follows:
- herbivore migrations;
- seed/grass cycles;
- fruiting seasons;
- reliable water;
- floodplain productivity.

The Great Plains are not one tribe or one continuous dense settlement. They contain a broad network of related communities sharing seasonal routes, marriage networks, hunting knowledge, plant knowledge, meeting places, stories, and language continuums.

### Family and social life
Family remains an important intimate unit, but it does not control all daily activity. People may sleep with family while spending much of the day in hunting, gathering, scouting, crafting, or travel groups.

Children are raised within the broader community. Very young children may remain near anchor communities; older children increasingly accompany mobile groups as they become capable.

### Hospitality
A peaceful traveler receives temporary protection and hospitality. The guest is expected not to endanger or exploit the host community.

### Leadership
Authority is distributed and role-based. Hunt leaders, scout leaders, craft leaders, migration leaders, and resource leaders hold authority appropriate to the problem. Trust is the central currency of authority.

### Conflict
Disputes begin within relevant work groups and may result in restitution, temporary loss of authority, reassignment, removal from dangerous responsibility, loss of trust, or extreme exclusion.

### Cultural principle
> **If you need shelter today, you may be the person providing it tomorrow.**

> **Don't get caught slipping. Trust has to be earned every day.**

---

## 10. MOUNTAINS

### Core social character
The extended specialist household/lineage is the core social unit. Family, household, teaching institution, and specialist knowledge repository overlap.

Mountain settlements are modeled around communities of approximately **50–70 people**. Specialist houses maintain related competencies rather than necessarily being one narrow occupation.

The exact number and composition of specialist houses are not fixed here.

### Specialist houses
Authority and knowledge are strongly tied to specialist houses and lineage heads. Houses coordinate:
- training;
- specialist knowledge;
- relevant resources;
- marriages;
- internal disputes;
- representation at the Mountain Conclave.

### Partnership
The established Mountain foundation favors partnership within the same specialist occupation/lineage in order to preserve and strengthen specialized knowledge across generations.

House heads coordinate marriageable populations among communities. The annual Mountain Conclave exchanges marriageable partners and balances specialist populations among villages.

Residence normally follows where the shared specialist skill is most needed.

### Avoiding close kin marriage
Mountain communities possess practical cultural knowledge that excessively close-family marriage produces poor outcomes. Marriage exchanges therefore cycle among villages and families rather than repeatedly pairing the same close groups.

### Leadership
Mountain authority is hierarchical within specialist lineages and collaborative between lineages. The village coordinates through house heads. The annual Mountain Conclave handles regional marriage exchange, specialist population balancing, knowledge exchange, trade, and major regional problems.

### Cultural principle
> **The house preserves the knowledge. The village preserves the people.**

## 11. RIVER

### Core social character
River society is organized around extended households embedded in a much larger river-corridor social network.

Working household structure includes parents, children, grandparents, siblings, cousins, and close kin, commonly around 10–20 people.

### Kinship
River kinship is bilateral. Children belong to both parents' families. Family relationships can extend many settlements upstream and downstream.

A broader category of **river kin** exists among communities with long-standing relationships. A traveler from a known river-connected community may receive river-kin treatment even without close biological relationship.

### Partnership
Individuals have meaningful input into partnerships. Families and communities consider age, health, kinship, family relationships, community needs, and river connections. Both prospective partners can refuse.

Marriage networks follow river corridors rather than occupational specialization.

### Residence
Couples may remain in one partner's community, move to the other, or establish a new household where practical. Moving does not sever the original family relationship.

### Leadership
River authority is situational, with strong elder and knowledge-holder influence. River communities use distributed coordination rather than centralized river-wide government.

### Conflict
River justice emphasizes restitution and restoration of relationships. Reputation travels upstream and downstream, making trust a major social resource.

### Cultural principle
> **The river has many bends, but it remains one river.**

> **A broken relationship travels farther than a broken boat.**

## 12. WETLANDS

### Core social character
Wetland society is **communal separatist**.

The primary daily unit is a substantially self-contained functional group of roughly **12–15 people**. A village contains several such groups.

Groups are independent without being isolated. They share surplus, lend labor, compensate for failed hunts or gathering, assist with major tasks, and support one another after injury or loss.

### Functional structure
A group can contain:
- hunters;
- gatherers;
- fishers;
- weavers;
- toolmakers;
- animal handlers;
- scouts/watchers;
- food processors.

### Partnership
Partnerships connect the broader village or nearby groups rather than being restricted to one's own functional group. This prevents the small groups from becoming closed social/genetic islands.

### Children
Children belong to their family and functional group while being known and supported by the whole village. They learn through observation, small tasks, games, work rotation, and interaction with adults outside their immediate group.

### Leadership
Leadership is temporary, functional, and group-centered. The most knowledgeable person leads the relevant activity. Inter-group decisions are coordinated by representatives of affected groups rather than permanent rulers.

### Conflict
Restoration is preferred. Possible consequences include repayment, replacement labor, temporary reassignment/separation, loss of trust, and removal from responsibilities. Permanent exclusion is reserved for people who repeatedly endanger the group or deliberately undermine communal survival.

### Cultural principle
> **Keep your group alive. Keep the village alive.**

> **Your group stands on its own, but your choices affect the village.**

## 13. DESERT

### Core social character
Desert society is organized around extended family households within oasis communities, connected by the larger **Lifekeeper network**.

### Lifekeepers
Lifekeepers are trained mobile specialists responsible for movement, environmental knowledge, hunting, route knowledge, distant conditions, and connecting oases.

Lifekeeper status is not a hereditary ruling caste. It is based on demonstrated competence and training.

### Water Watchers
Water Watchers safeguard the usability of oasis water by:
- checking water quality;
- monitoring levels;
- maintaining collection points;
- protecting drinking areas from contamination;
- maintaining emergency reserves;
- coordinating rationing during scarcity.

They regulate **use**, not ownership or access. Life-saving water is not denied to a stranded traveler, though quantities may be limited during scarcity.

### Family and partnership
Partnerships commonly connect different oasis communities. Couples may remain in one oasis or move according to family needs, community needs, water/resource conditions, and travel obligations.

Children belong to their family and oasis community. Unusual aptitude for travel, hunting, endurance, judgment, or environmental awareness may lead toward Lifekeeper training.

### Authority
Oasis elders guide family/community decisions. Water Watchers hold specialized authority over sustainable water use. Lifekeepers hold specialized practical authority over travel and environmental danger.

No single authority controls the entire society.

### Cultural principle
> **Water is shared, but survival requires discipline.**

> **Those who know the danger have a responsibility to warn the people.**

---

## 14. COAST

### Core social character
The coastal community itself functions as the practical household. Coastal people live communally rather than primarily in separate nuclear-family households.

### Housing
Large communal structures contain shared sleeping areas. Most adults sleep together in common space. Children have a protected section. Ordinary individual bedrooms do not exist.

Enclosed private spaces exist for genuinely private activities, including reproduction and intimate partnership. These are community spaces rather than personal property.

### Parentage and children
Biological parentage is recognized, but parentage does not create exclusive responsibility. Children are raised communally and can maintain strong individual bonds with parents and other adults.

Adults collectively supervise, teach, discipline, feed, protect, train, and support children.

### Partnership
Partnerships exist inside the communal structure and do not automatically create separate households. The community remains the primary domestic unit.

### Leadership
Coastal authority is communal and competence-based. Task coordinators organize fishing, construction, food preservation, defense, child learning, and other activities without becoming rulers.

Major decisions seek broad communal agreement. Elders have substantial advisory influence based on accumulated knowledge.

### Conflict
Serious disputes are brought before respected adults/elders. Consequences emphasize apology, restitution, repair work, temporary removal from responsibilities, separation, and communal loss of trust.

### Cultural principle
> **Almost everything is shared; privacy exists when it is needed.**

> **Nobody rules the community; the community carries itself.**

# PART IV — SHARED CHILDHOOD / DEVELOPMENT FOUNDATION

## 15. Developmental Principle

Children across Hearth are not treated as miniature adults. Childhood is a period of observation, play, gradual responsibility, skill acquisition, and integration into the community.

The six peoples nevertheless express this through different social structures.

### Plains
Children learn through observation, participation, play, and gradual inclusion in mobile work. Skills develop according to developmental stage and community need.

### Mountains
Children are embedded in specialist lineages and learn from multiple adults possessing the lineage's skills. Training is strongly connected to specialist knowledge.

### River
Children grow within extended families while acquiring practical knowledge from household and river-community networks.

### Wetlands
Children learn through group life, task rotation, games, observation, and interaction with adults outside their immediate functional group.

### Desert
Children learn oasis survival, water awareness, environmental knowledge, and community responsibilities. Some later enter Lifekeeper training through aptitude and demonstrated capability.

### Coast
Children are raised communally and participate in generalist education, food knowledge, coastal safety, labor, and community life.

# PART V — CURRENT CULTURAL CROSS-CONNECTIONS

## 16. Cultural Boundaries Are Not Hard Political Borders

Hearth is the most connected of the major continents. Cultural regions overlap.

River and grassland corridors carry language, marriage, technology, ecological knowledge, and stories. Mountains, difficult wetlands, dry interiors, and isolated coasts can produce stronger local identities.

A geographic region does not automatically equal one people.

## 17. Major Cross-Cultural Relationships

Established material includes significant interaction among:
- Plains and River populations;
- Plains and Wetlands populations;
- River and Coast populations;
- River and Wetlands populations;
- Desert oases through Lifekeeper networks;
- Mountain communities through the Mountain Conclave and specialist exchange.

These relationships are part of Hearth's cultural architecture rather than exceptions to it.
