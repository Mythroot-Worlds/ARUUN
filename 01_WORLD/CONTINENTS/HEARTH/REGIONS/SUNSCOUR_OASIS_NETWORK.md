# HEARTH — SUNSCOUR OASIS NETWORK
## Regional Hydrology / Human Geography Layer v0.1

**Status:** WORKING CANON — SCIENCE-DERIVED GEOGRAPHIC DECISION  
**Purpose:** Determine where permanent and seasonal desert water should occur before finalizing Sunscour settlement and culture.

---

## 1. Source Logic

Existing Hearth canon establishes Sunscour as a major **closed/endorheic basin**.

Working drainage:

**surrounding mountain/upland catchments → seasonal/perennial rivers → inland lake or salt basin → evaporation**

The existing hydrology layer already requires:
- scattered permanent water;
- seasonal lakes;
- springs;
- groundwater-fed oases;
- mineral/salt accumulation;
- sparse interior population;
- population concentration around reliable water.

The climate layer establishes that Hearth's dry interiors arise primarily through **continentality and rain-shadow effects**.

The human-geography rule is:

**food productivity + reliable water + tolerable climate + manageable terrain + safe access + seasonal predictability → population concentration**

This layer turns those broad rules into a regional water pattern.

---

# 2. Scientific Oasis Rule

Oases are **not randomly distributed green dots**.

They form where the groundwater system makes surface discharge or shallow groundwater likely.

The most favorable locations are:

1. **Mountain-front alluvial fans**
   - Mountain precipitation recharges groundwater.
   - Water infiltrates coarse sediments.
   - Groundwater can move toward the basin floor.
   - Springs/seeps become likely where the aquifer meets lower-permeability material.

2. **Fault zones**
   - Faults can act as groundwater conduits or barriers.
   - Springs can cluster along faults and especially at fault intersections.

3. **Canyon mouths and valley constrictions**
   - Concentrate runoff and groundwater flow.

4. **Contacts between permeable and impermeable rock**
   - Groundwater can be forced toward the surface.

5. **Playa/inland-basin margins**
   - Shallow groundwater can produce spring mounds, marshy margins, or persistent vegetation.
   - The basin center itself is more likely to become saline/ephemeral than reliably potable.

6. **Inland drainage convergence**
   - Seasonal rivers can create localized shallow groundwater and temporary wetlands.

Earth analogues support this pattern: USGS describes desert springs and oases forming where groundwater reaches the surface, often in valley bottoms or shallow slopes, with recharge commonly originating in surrounding mountains and groundwater flow frequently controlled by faults. # 3. Sunscour Water Architecture

Sunscour should **not** be an evenly dry bowl.

It should contain a hierarchy:

### Tier 1 — Major perennial oasis systems
Reliable enough to support repeated or permanent human occupation.

**Working count: 5 major systems.**

### Tier 2 — Secondary spring/groundwater systems
Reliable in normal years but smaller or more localized.

**Working count: 6–10 systems.**

### Tier 3 — Seasonal water
Ephemeral streams, temporary pools, rain-fed depressions, seasonal lakes, and post-storm wetlands.

**Numerous.**

### Tier 4 — Emergency/route water
Small seeps, rock pools, retained runoff, shallow groundwater pockets, and old water sites known to experienced travelers.

**Many, but unreliable and highly knowledge-dependent.**

This gives Sunscour a real hydrologic network without turning it into a desert full of permanent green villages.

# 4. MAJOR OASIS SYSTEMS

The following are **relative map placements**, not arbitrary coordinates. They should be placed according to the existing Sunscour basin, surrounding ranges, and drainage geometry.

## OASIS 01 — NORTHWEST MOUNTAIN-FRONT OASIS

**Position:** Western/northwestern Sunscour margin where Stonehearth/Upland drainage approaches the basin.

**Formation:**
- recharge from uplands;
- coarse alluvial-fan deposits;
- groundwater moving basinward;
- discharge where basin sediments become less permeable.

**Character:**
A chain of springs and shallow wet ground rather than one circular "pond."

**Human use:**
One of the most likely early permanent/semi-permanent settlement zones.

**Cultural effect:**
Strong connection between upland travelers and basin communities.

---

## OASIS 02 — NORTHEAST AEGIR FOOTHILL OASIS

**Position:** Northern/northeastern Sunscour margin below the Aegir mountain system.

**Formation:**
- high-elevation recharge;
- steep mountain-front runoff;
- buried alluvial channels;
- fault-controlled groundwater discharge.

**Character:**
A linear or clustered spring zone along the mountain front.

**Human use:**
Major staging point for movement between Aegir foothills and Sunscour.

**Cultural effect:**
Likely one of the strongest places for exchange between mountain-adjacent and desert peoples.

---

## OASIS 03 — EASTERN FAULT-LINE OASIS

**Position:** Eastern Sunscour margin where major basin faults or structural boundaries intersect.

**Formation:**
Fault-controlled upward groundwater movement.

**Character:**
Several springs/seeps distributed along a structural line rather than a single spring.

**Human use:**
Potentially supports a string of small settlements or one larger community plus seasonal camps.

**Cultural effect:**
Creates a predictable north-south travel corridor.

---

## OASIS 04 — SOUTHERN RANGE-FOOT OASIS

**Position:** Northern face/foothill margin of the Southward Range where runoff and groundwater feed the closed basin.

**Formation:**
- mountain recharge;
- alluvial fans;
- groundwater accumulation;
- geological contact zones.

**Character:**
A greener mountain-foot corridor grading into desert scrub.

**Human use:**
Important seasonal refuge and travel route.

**Cultural effect:**
Potential connection between southern foothill and Sunscour communities.

---

## OASIS 05 — SOUTHWESTERN CANYON / FAN OASIS

**Position:** Southwestern Sunscour margin near mountain-front canyon outlets.

**Formation:**
Concentrated runoff and groundwater discharge where canyon systems open onto basin-floor sediments.

**Character:**
Smaller than the major northern/eastern systems but potentially exceptionally productive.

**Human use:**
Likely supports a smaller permanent community plus mobile groups.

**Cultural effect:**
Potentially more isolated and locally specialized.

---

# 5. SECONDARY OASIS SYSTEMS

Place secondary springs preferentially:

- along the downstream ends of major alluvial fans;
- at fault intersections;
- where seasonal river channels cross structural boundaries;
- around basin-margin depressions;
- at canyon mouths;
- where shallow groundwater approaches the surface.

These should form **clusters and corridors**, not a random grid.

Some should fail during severe drought years.

Some should expand after unusually wet years.

---

# 6. THE BASIN CENTER

The deepest portion of Sunscour should **not** be dominated by freshwater oases.

Instead expect:

- saline lake;
- salt flats;
- mudflats/playas;
- ephemeral lake expansion;
- mineral crusts;
- seasonal marsh around freshwater inflows;
- groundwater-fed spring mounds in selected locations.

The center is a destination for:
- salt/mineral collection;
- seasonal animal concentrations;
- rare water events;
- ecological pulses;

rather than the best location for permanent human population.

This keeps the closed-basin logic intact.

---

# 7. RIVER BEHAVIOR

Sunscour rivers should be highly variable.

### Mountain zones
More reliable flow.

### Fan zones
Water spreads into gravel/sediment and may disappear underground.

### Basin floor
Channels become intermittent, braided, distributary, or terminal.

### Near the terminal basin
Water may:
- evaporate;
- infiltrate;
- form seasonal lakes;
- feed marshes;
- become saline.

A "river" on the map should therefore not imply year-round surface water along its entire length.

---

# 8. HUMAN SETTLEMENT CONSEQUENCES

Sunscour's **105,000-person dry-interior allocation** remains compatible with a sparse landscape because those people are concentrated around the water network rather than evenly distributed.

Expected pattern:

**major oasis → 50–120 person community**

surrounded by:

**seasonal camps → route camps → hunting/gathering territories → nearly empty extreme desert**

Several oasis communities may be connected by traveling groups.

No assumption is made that every major oasis becomes a permanent village.

---

# 9. NOMADIC VS. STATIONARY RESULT

The geography produces a **mixed desert society**.

### Oasis communities
Relatively stationary or semi-stationary.

They provide:
- stable food;
- children/elder support;
- storage;
- crafts;
- animal care;
- refuge.

### Desert travelers
Highly mobile.

They provide:
- distant resource knowledge;
- hunting;
- route knowledge;
- news;
- inter-oasis connections;
- warnings about changing water conditions.

Neither lifestyle is complete without the other.

The Desert people therefore become a **network culture rather than a purely nomadic or purely oasis culture**.

---

# 10. WATER KNOWLEDGE

Water knowledge becomes a major cultural asset.

Different communities can know different parts of the network.

Knowledge includes:
- reliable vs. unreliable springs;
- seasonal timing;
- water quality;
- route distance;
- hidden seep locations;
- emergency water;
- safe camping;
- storm runoff;
- animal watering points.

Some water sources can be broadly known.

Others can be local knowledge.

A source can be physically real without being equally accessible to every traveler.

---

# 11. SCIENTIFIC CHECK

This pattern is intentionally based on real desert hydrogeology:

**mountains receive precipitation → recharge infiltrates → groundwater travels through aquifers/alluvial deposits → geology redirects flow → springs/seeps appear at discharge zones → vegetation concentrates around those water sources.**

USGS examples show that desert spring systems can persist as biologically rich oases and that faults can strongly control where springs occur. citeturn0search0turn0search6turn0search7

# 12. WHAT REMAINS OPEN

Still intentionally undecided:
- exact oasis names;
- exact spring flow rates;
- exact community populations;
- exact seasonal lake size;
- exact aquifer depth;
- exact fault traces;

---

# DESIGN PRINCIPLE

**Do not place water where people need it.**

Place water where **geology and climate create it**.

Then place people where the resulting water network makes survival plausible.

# HEARTH — HYDROLOGY & DRAINAGE
## v0.2 Sunscour Oasis Update

**Status:** WORKING CANON — ADDITIVE REGIONAL DETAIL

This revision preserves the v0.1 continental drainage architecture and adds the first science-derived local water pattern for Sunscour Basin.

## Existing continental rule

Hearth follows:

**mountains/uplands → headwaters → tributaries → rivers → floodplains/wetlands/lakes → deltas/estuaries**

Sunscour remains an **endorheic basin**:

**mountain catchments → seasonal/perennial rivers → inland lake/salt basin → evaporation**

## Sunscour regional hydrology

The basin is now modeled as a groundwater-connected desert rather than uniformly dry terrain.

### Major perennial oasis systems
Five major oasis systems are established as working placements:

1. Northwest mountain-front / Stonehearth transition
2. Northeast Aegir foothill margin
3. Eastern fault-line spring corridor
4. Southern Southward Range foothill margin
5. Southwestern canyon/fan margin

### Secondary systems
Six to ten smaller spring/groundwater systems should occur around:
- alluvial-fan toes;
- fault intersections;
- canyon mouths;
- permeable/impermeable geological contacts;
- seasonal channel crossings;
- basin-margin depressions.

### Seasonal water
Numerous intermittent streams, temporary pools, seasonal lakes, runoff depressions, and post-storm wetlands remain distributed through the basin.

### Basin center
The lowest central areas are dominated by:
- saline lake;
- salt flats/playas;
- mudflats;
- seasonal lake expansion;
- mineral accumulation;
- selected freshwater spring mounds around groundwater discharge zones.

The central basin is not a freshwater oasis field.

## Scientific basis

Desert oases commonly form where groundwater reaches the surface through springs or seeps. Groundwater is frequently recharged in surrounding mountains and redirected by faults, rock permeability contrasts, and basin-fill deposits. USGS studies document these mechanisms in multiple arid basins. citeturn0search0turn0search2turn0search7

## Human geography consequence

Sunscour's 105,000-person dry-interior allocation remains sparse overall but concentrated around the oasis network.

The expected settlement structure is:

**major oasis → semi-stationary community**

surrounded by:

**seasonal camps → travel corridors → hunting/gathering territories → extreme low-density desert**

This supports a mixed Desert culture of oasis communities and mobile travelers.

## Mapping rule

Do not place oases because a settlement needs one.

Place them where:
**mountain recharge + groundwater flow + basin geology + structural discharge + local terrain**
make them plausible.

People follow the resulting water network.
