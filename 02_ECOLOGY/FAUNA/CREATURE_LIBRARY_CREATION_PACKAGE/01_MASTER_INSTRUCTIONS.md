# MASTER CREATURE-BUILDING INSTRUCTIONS

## What we are building
Think **representative evolutionary vocabulary**, not a complete bestiary.

A writer should recognize that this world contains:
- large prehistoric-feeling herbivores;
- dangerous reptilian/serpentine predators;
- heavy-bodied beasts;
- amphibious animals;
- scavengers;
- burrowers;
- aquatic predators;
- aerial organisms;
- unusual Lost lineages.

## Function comes first
Every creature begins with an ecological job: grazer, browser, prey, omnivore, scavenger, predator, ecosystem engineer, burrower, filter feeder, aquatic grazer, marine predator, aerial consumer, aerial predator, migratory connector, etc.

Start with:
> What ecological problem needs solving?

Then build the organism that solves it.

## Use the Predictive Evolution Matrix
Choose:
1. CONTINENT
2. CLIMATE
3. FUNCTION

Then generate evolutionary possibilities and classify them:
- FAVORED
- PLAUSIBLE
- DIFFICULT
- POOR FIT

The matrix defines the scientifically defensible design space; it does not dictate the final creature.

## Science is the boundary, not the prison
Use science to determine plausibility, pressures, and tradeoffs. Do not eliminate every interesting idea. If a deliberate creative choice is scientifically unusual, identify it rather than pretending it is a scientific prediction.

## Prehistoric identity
Default toward:
- large reptiles;
- serpentine predators;
- heavy herbivores;
- armored animals;
- tusked/horned beasts;
- burrowers;
- amphibious predators;
- egg-layers;
- primitive-feeling aerial forms;
- large arthropods where justified;
- tails, claws, scales, plates, crests, beaks, robust jaws.

But do NOT make everything a dinosaur. Fur, feathers, filaments, mammal-like forms, etc. are allowed when supported.

The test is:
> Does it feel prehistoric because of ecology, anatomy, behavior, and evolutionary history?

## Convergent evolution
Do not avoid resemblance to Earth animals. A creature may independently resemble a crocodilian, tyrannosaur, snake, boar, bison, bird, rodent, etc. If it does, explain the functional reason.

## Tradeoffs
Good creatures are not good at everything. For every major advantage, ask:
> What did it give up to get it?

Examples: excellent swimming/poor land mobility; huge body/high resource needs; armor/reduced speed; specialization/narrow diet; powerful senses/energy cost.

## Climate is not decoration
Consider water, vegetation, seasonality, temperature, flooding/drought, terrain, resources, predators, migration, reproduction.

## Continental logic
Hearth: connected ecosystems, broad ranges, long migrations, large populations.
Shattered: fragmentation, isolation, regional divergence, body-size shifts, coastal dependence.
Rift: extreme transitions, elevation, freshwater, wetlands, volcanic disturbance, specialization.
Lost: deep-time independent evolution, adaptive radiation, convergence, unusual body plans, ancient lineages.

Lost should be different because its evolutionary history is different, not because its physics are alien.

## Distribution
Do not make every continent completely unique. Hearth/Shattered/Rift may share ancestral lineages with regional variants. Lost may be much more divergent.

## Don't overdesign
A creature earns inclusion when it:
- fills a missing essential function;
- demonstrates a new evolutionary solution;
- makes a region distinct;
- establishes a major visual/ecological anchor;
- or provides a useful recurring species.

Stop when additions become mostly cosmetic variants.

## Size discipline
Large is encouraged; routine kaiju are not. Favor creatures impressive enough to seriously threaten humans but still biologically manageable. Avoid routine 30–60 foot monsters without exceptional ecological justification.

## Canonical vs background
Mark each:
- CANONICAL EXEMPLAR — fully developed, recurring reference.
- BACKGROUND ARCHETYPE — establishes that related organisms exist without requiring a full species entry.

## Naming
Working names are fine during development. Do not name the world.
