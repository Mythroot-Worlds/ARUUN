# STYLE, FEEL, AND BIOLOGICAL IDENTITY

## Desired feeling
An **old, old world**: deep time, dangerous ecosystems, abundant life, robust vegetation, large animals, primitive-looking but functional anatomy, evolutionary experimentation, and the beginning of civilization.

Avoid:
- Star Wars-style alien worlds;
- generic fantasy bestiaries;
- monster manuals;
- Jurassic Park with renamed animals;
- creatures that exist only to look cool.

## Visual vocabulary
Encourage strong forms:
low/broad bodies; heavy limbs; tails; claws; scales; armor; horns/tusks; crests; beaks; elongated bodies; powerful jaws.

Also use fur, feathers, filaments, skin, plates, mixed coverings, and unusual textures when biologically justified.

## "T-Rex but not T-Rex" principle
If evolution independently produces a tyrannosaur-like large terrestrial predator, that is allowed. Ask why it is shaped that way, what it hunts, what senses it uses, what it sacrifices, and what its lineage is.

A familiar silhouette can be scientifically grounded.

## Big but not absurd
Impressive scale is encouraged without routine kaiju. A creature can be 10–20 feet long, several hundred kilograms, or dangerous enough to threaten a human group and still feel enormous.

## Weirdness rule
Weirdness should come from different evolutionary history, ecological pressure, convergence, tradeoffs, isolation, or specialization—not random body parts.

Avoid arbitrary extra heads/limbs or decorative anatomy without function unless the world explicitly establishes a reason.

## "Why does it have that?" test
Every unusual feature should answer:
> What does this feature do?

## Earth resemblance test
Similarity to Earth is not failure. If it results from convergent evolution or shared ecological constraints, keep it.

## Too-fantasy test
Reject or revise concepts relying primarily on supernatural anatomy, impossible physiology, unexplained powers, or arbitrary monster design during this biology pass.
