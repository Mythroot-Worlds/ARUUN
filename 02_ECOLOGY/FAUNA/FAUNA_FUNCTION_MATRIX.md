# Mythroot Prehistoric World — Faunal Function Matrix v0.1

**Status:** Broad working framework. Not locked canon.

## Core principle

Do not begin by asking:

> "What animals should exist?"

Begin by asking:

> **"What ecological work needs to be done?"**

A creature belongs in the world because it occupies a meaningful ecological role, not merely because its body plan looks cool.

**A giant lizard is absolutely allowed.** It just needs a reason to exist.

---

# I. PRIMARY PRODUCERS

The foundation of the food web.

### Terrestrial producers
Possible functions:
- ground-cover plants;
- grasses/grass analogues;
- shrubs;
- low forests;
- fruit/seed producers;
- large woody vegetation;
- fast-colonizing plants after disturbance.

### Aquatic producers
Possible functions:
- algae;
- floating plants;
- submerged vegetation;
- shoreline plants;
- wetland producers.

### Design question
What plant communities capture energy in each climate, and which animals have evolved to exploit them?

---

# II. SMALL CONSUMERS

These turn plant and microbial production into animal biomass.

Possible functions:
- grazers;
- browsers;
- seed eaters;
- fruit eaters;
- root/tuber specialists;
- insect/arthropod hunters;
- aquatic micro-feeders;
- filter feeders.

These do not need to be visually impressive. They make the ecosystem work.

---

# III. MID-LEVEL CONSUMERS

The enormous middle layer that makes an ecosystem feel alive.

Possible functions:
- small/medium predators;
- omnivores;
- scavengers;
- amphibious hunters;
- burrowing predators;
- nest/egg predators;
- opportunistic feeders.

This category should contain many of the animals humans regularly encounter.

---

# IV. LARGE HERBIVORES

These are some of the most important prehistoric-world animals.

### Grazers
Shape open vegetation and create migration corridors.

### Browsers
Control shrubs, young trees, and forest edges.

### Mixed feeders
Move between grassland, woodland, and seasonal vegetation.

### Aquatic/semi-aquatic herbivores
Modify wetlands and waterways.

### Heavy generalists
Consume enormous amounts of vegetation and physically reshape habitats.

**Design principle:** Large herbivores are not merely "big animals." Their feeding behavior should change the landscape.

---

# V. MAJOR PREDATORS

We need multiple hunting strategies rather than simply increasingly large carnivores.

### Ambush predators
- rely on concealment;
- exploit terrain or water;
- can be dangerous without enormous body size.

### Pursuit predators
- endurance or burst speed;
- often target weakened or isolated prey.

### Pack/social predators
- individually manageable;
- collectively dangerous;
- create strong pressure for group behavior in prey and humans.

### Territorial predators
- defend a specific resource-rich area;
- dangerous because humans can accidentally enter their territory.

### Aquatic predators
- control waterways;
- make drinking crossings dangerous;
- interact strongly with fishing and settlement.

### Specialist predators
Target a particular prey type, habitat, or behavior.

---

# VI. SCAVENGERS

Critical nutrient recyclers.

Possible functions:
- large carcass scavengers;
- small carrion feeders;
- bone processors;
- aquatic scavengers;
- opportunistic omnivores.

Scavengers should create competition around kills and influence human hunting behavior.

---

# VII. ECOSYSTEM ENGINEERS

These are disproportionately important.

An ecosystem engineer physically changes its environment.

Possible examples:
- dam/building animals;
- burrowers;
- tunnelers;
- vegetation-clearing herbivores;
- mud/soil manipulators;
- reef/colony builders;
- animals that create wallows or watering holes;
- enormous grazers that maintain open country.

**Design question:**

> If this animal disappeared, what would physically change?

If the answer is "not much," it may not be an ecosystem-engineer candidate.

---

# VIII. POLLINATORS AND DISPERSERS

These connect plants to animals and environments.

Possible functions:
- pollen transfer;
- seed dispersal;
- fruit dispersal;
- long-distance plant migration;
- specialized plant partnerships.

They can be insects, arthropods, flying vertebrates, climbing animals, or completely unfamiliar lineages.

---

# IX. MIGRATORY CONNECTORS

Some organisms connect ecosystems across enormous distances.

Possible functions:
- seasonal grazing migrations;
- spawning migrations;
- coastal/inland movement;
- vertical mountain migrations;
- flying migrations;
- seasonal predator following.

These are particularly important for Hearth's connected ecology.

---

# X. AQUATIC GUILDS

"Fish" is not one ecological role.

Potential aquatic functions include:

- schooling prey;
- large solitary predators;
- bottom feeders;
- algae grazers;
- filter feeders;
- scavengers;
- freshwater specialists;
- marine specialists;
- amphibious feeders;
- migratory/spawning species.

Different continents can solve the same aquatic problem with different body plans.

---

# XI. BURROWERS / SHELTER MAKERS

Important in prehistoric environments because shelter is an ecosystem resource.

Functions:
- create underground habitats;
- aerate soil;
- provide refuge for other organisms;
- alter drainage;
- create nesting sites;
- escape predators.

Some may become enormous.

---

# XII. SMALL DANGEROUS THINGS

Not every serious threat needs to be megafauna.

Potential ecological roles:
- venomous predators;
- territorial swarm organisms;
- aggressive nest defenders;
- parasites;
- disease vectors;
- highly defensive herbivores;
- animals whose danger comes from numbers rather than size.

This category prevents the world from becoming "everything dangerous is huge."

---

# XIII. HUMAN-RELEVANT SPECIES

Some species matter because humans interact with them heavily.

Possible relationships:
- prey;
- dangerous predators;
- scavengers around settlements;
- domestication candidates;
- competition for food;
- useful hides/bones;
- medicinal relationships;
- seed/plant relationships;
- warning/sign species;
- seasonal indicators.

A creature does not need to be domesticated to be culturally important.

---

# XIV. THE "20 PEOPLE WITH SPEARS" SCALE

This is a useful narrative benchmark, not a numerical rule.

A dangerous large animal should often be:

**Too dangerous for one person.**

**Possible for a coordinated hunting group.**

**Potentially deadly even to that group if they make mistakes.**

This creates the desired prehistoric pressure without making every animal a kaiju.

The danger can come from:
- size;
- strength;
- armor;
- tusks/horns;
- speed;
- venom;
- constriction;
- ambush;
- pack behavior;
- terrain;
- intelligence;
- territorial aggression.

---

# XV. SHARED FAUNA VS. REGIONAL VARIANTS

The main three continental systems should share broad ancestral ecological roles.

For example, a large omnivorous lineage might exist across Hearth, Shattered, and Rift.

But:

**Hearth:** connected populations remain relatively similar.

**Shattered:** isolation creates regional variants.

**Rift:** wetlands, mountains, volcanism, and rapidly changing habitats produce specialized forms.

**Lost:** deep isolation allows an independent evolutionary trajectory.

The same ecological function may therefore be performed by:
- the same lineage;
- a regional descendant;
- a different lineage;
- or a convergently evolved organism.

---

# XVI. LOST CONTINENT RULE

Lost should not be designed as a catalogue of "weird animals."

Instead ask:

1. What ancestral lineages reached Lost before isolation?
2. Which survived?
3. Which disappeared?
4. Which ecological niches became vacant?
5. Which organisms expanded into those niches?
6. Which traits became advantageous under Lost's climates?
7. Did unrelated organisms converge on similar solutions?
8. Did ancient lineages persist because competition was absent?

The result should be **evolutionarily unfamiliar but ecologically understandable.**

---

# XVII. UNKNOWN ECOLOGY CREATION RULES

This is the beginning of the eventual creator-facing framework.

Anyone inventing a new organism should be able to answer:

### 1. What does it eat?
Be specific about the resource, not just "plants" or "meat."

### 2. What eats it?
Every major organism should have ecological relationships.

### 3. What does it compete with?
Identify overlapping resources or habitats.

### 4. What ecological problem does it solve?
What niche does it occupy?

### 5. What does it change?
Does it modify soil, vegetation, water, carcasses, nests, or other habitats?

### 6. Where can it survive?
Climate, terrain, water, elevation, and seasonal constraints.

### 7. How does it reproduce?
At broad ecological level; detailed reproductive biology is optional unless relevant.

### 8. How does it avoid being eaten?
Armor, speed, group behavior, camouflage, venom, size, habitat, etc.

### 9. Why does its body plan make sense?
Connect anatomy to environmental pressures.

### 10. What happens if it disappears?
This reveals whether it actually matters to the ecosystem.

---

# XVIII. CREATURE DESIGN TEST

A proposed organism should survive five questions:

**Niche:** What does it do?

**Energy:** Where does its food/energy come from?

**Pressure:** What keeps it from becoming impossibly successful?

**Relationships:** What depends on it, hunts it, competes with it, or benefits from it?

**History:** Why did this organism evolve here instead of somewhere else?

If those answers work, the creature can be as strange as we want.

---

# XIX. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

We are not attempting to recreate Earth's taxonomy.

We are using Earth as a library of demonstrated ecological solutions.

The world can contain:
- familiar-looking animals;
- unfamiliar body plans;
- convergent forms;
- ancient lineages;
- regional variants;
- giant animals;
- surprisingly small dangerous animals;
- organisms with no obvious Earth analogue.

The constraint is not:

> "Could I find this exact animal on Earth?"

The constraint is:

> **"Does this organism make ecological sense in this world?"**

---

# XX. CURRENT HORIZONTAL FAUNA CHECKLIST

Before designing individual species, every major continental system should eventually have broad representatives of:

- primary consumers;
- secondary consumers;
- large herbivores;
- major predators;
- scavengers;
- ecosystem engineers;
- pollinators/dispersers;
- aquatic guilds;
- burrowers;
- migratory species;
- dangerous small species;
- human-relevant species.

The next step is not to fill this list with species.

The next step is to compare the **four continents** against the matrix and identify which ecological functions are shared, which are regionally specialized, and which are unique to Lost.
