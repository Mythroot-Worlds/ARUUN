# ARUUNITE EDIBLE FLORA EXPANSION HANDOFF

## Purpose

Expand the existing Aruunite flora library with enough edible and food-supporting plant species to make prehistoric human survival on Aruun biologically credible.

This is **not** a complete botanical catalog. Build enough staple foods, seasonal foods, fallback foods, processed foods, fermentation substrates, and culturally important plants that a writer can plausibly answer, “What do these people eat here?” without defaulting to Earth crops.

## Core rule

Create plants from:

**ecological niche → plant function → human use → regional distribution → seasonality → processing requirements**

Do not start with a food name and work backward.

## Food categories to cover

### 1. Starchy grains / grass seeds
Include annual or perennial grass seeds, large-seeded wild grasses, pseudo-grains, wetland grains, drought-tolerant grains, and upland/cold-tolerant grains.

Track harvest season, raw usability, grinding/pounding, cooking, storage, yield, habitat, and domestication potential.

Do not simply rename wheat, oats, barley, or rice.

### 2. Seeds and nuts
Include tree nuts, ground nuts, oily seeds, hard-shelled seeds, and soft seeds. Prioritize fat, protein, calories, and storage value.

### 3. Roots, tubers, bulbs, rhizomes, and corms
Include dependable calorie sources and fallback foods. Some may require cooking, soaking, leaching, drying, or fermentation to become safe.

### 4. Fruits
Use multiple strategies: high-sugar, large fleshy, small abundant, dry, acidic, and water-rich fruits. Include trees, shrubs, vines, ground plants, wetland fruits, forest fruits, and dryland fruits.

### 5. Legume / pulse analogues
Include protein-rich seeds, pods, beans, lentil-like seeds, and climbing legumes. Some should require soaking, boiling, roasting, or fermentation.

### 6. Leafy greens
Useful mainly for micronutrients, seasonal diversity, medicine, and flavor. Include leaves, shoots, young fronds, and aquatic greens.

### 7. Edible flowers / shoots / stems
Secondary foods, flavorings, ritual foods, and medicines. Do not overproduce minor vegetables.

### 8. Mushrooms / fungal foods
A modest number of ecologically distinct edible fungi. Include at least one recognizable, seasonally abundant traditional food and some dangerous/difficult relatives.

## Processing plants

Deliberately include plants whose edible parts can become:
- flour or meal
- porridge
- cakes or flatbreads
- starch
- dried fruit
- preserves
- nut/seed pastes
- oils
- roasted seeds
- fermented foods
- fermented beverages

Processing difficulty matters. Highly mobile groups should not depend on foods requiring excessive processing unless the ecological payoff justifies it.

## Fermentation

Include plausible plant substrates for fermentation:
- sugar-rich fruits
- grain/seed starches
- roots/tubers

Fermentation can exist before agriculture when substrates and knowledge make it practical. Agriculture increases reliability and volume; it does not invent fermentation.

## Honey-like sweetener

Coordinate with fauna creation to provide at least one nectar-plant + nectar-collecting insect system capable of producing a concentrated stored carbohydrate resembling honey.

It should provide:
- concentrated sugar
- preservation value
- fermentation potential
- cultural/ritual value

Do not make it universally abundant.

## Plant oils and fats

Include oil-rich seeds, nuts, fruit oils, and extractable plant fats. Track crushing, heating, pressing, water separation, or roasting requirements.

## Hydration plants

Especially in hot/arid regions, include water-rich fruits, succulent storage tissues, or moisture-rich stems. These supplement rather than replace dependable water sources.

## Saline/mineral foods

Where ecologically appropriate, include plants that concentrate salts or minerals and can be useful in saline basins or coastal environments.

## Regional coverage

### Hearth
Prioritize grass/seed staples, woodland nuts, fruits, river/floodplain plants, wetland foods, temperate roots, and seasonal greens.

### Rift
Prioritize lake/wetland foods, tropical fruits, roots/tubers, grass seeds, dry-season fallback foods, highland grains/seeds, and water-rich foods.

### Shattered
Prioritize coastal fruits, island nuts/seeds, wet forest foods, seasonal basin plants, dry-island fallback foods, and plants suited to repeated human movement.

### Lost
Give this the greatest botanical novelty: independent Lost lineages, unfamiliar but functional edible structures, deep-interior staples, specialized arid foods, unusual highland plants, wetland foods, coastal resources, and plants without close analogues on the other continents.

Lost should feel botanically different, **not randomly alien**.

## Wild food vs. domestication

Give every important food plant a rough rating:

- **0 — Gathered only**
- **1 — Managed wild resource**
- **2 — Proto-domestication candidate**
- **3 — Strong domestication candidate**
- **4 — Likely future staple**

Do not assume agriculture exists merely because a plant is domestication-compatible.

## Toxicity and preparation

Include some plants that are:
- edible only after cooking
- edible only after leaching/soaking
- seasonally toxic
- edible in one part but toxic in another
- medicinal at low doses but dangerous at high doses
- visually similar to edible species

Do not make everything poisonous.

## Seasonality

Track:
- primary harvest season
- secondary/backup season
- storage potential
- whether the edible part remains useful after maturation

Seasonality should influence migration, group size, food sharing, preservation, fermentation, settlement, and eventual cultivation.

## Flora-fauna relationships

Include plants that:
- feed both humans and megafauna
- depend on animal seed dispersal
- grow after grazing or fire
- benefit from animal disturbance
- are defended by megafauna
- support the honey-like insect system

Flora is part of the food web, not a supermarket inventory.

## Do not overbuild

We do not need hundreds of edible plants immediately.

We need enough that:
1. every major human region has dependable plant foods;
2. seasonal gaps make sense;
3. staple calories exist;
4. protein and fat supplementation exists;
5. fermentation is possible;
6. processing has meaningful options;
7. future agriculture has plausible candidates;
8. writers can invent meals without contradicting ecology.

## Required entry for every canonical edible plant

### Identity
- Working name
- Plant type
- Continent(s)
- Environment(s)

### Food
- Edible part
- Main nutritional/calorie contribution
- Raw/cooked/processed uses
- Taste/aroma
- Storage potential

### Ecology
- Growth form
- Water requirement
- Soil/habitat
- Seasonality
- Major animal relationships

### Human use
- Gathered / managed / domestication candidate
- Processing requirements
- Preservation
- Fermentation potential
- Oil/sweetener/starch potential

### Safety
- Toxicity
- Required preparation
- Look-alike hazards

### Cultural potential
- Staple / seasonal / luxury / emergency / medicinal / ritual
- Importance to mobile vs. sedentary populations

### Rationale
A concise explanation of why the plant exists where it does and why Aruunites would care about it.

## Final quality test

Before adding a plant, ask:

> **Would Aruunites actually care about this plant enough to learn, harvest, protect, move toward, process, trade, cultivate, or remember it?**

If not, keep it as background flora rather than a canonical edible species.

The goal is **a believable prehistoric food ecology**, not a list of edible nouns.
