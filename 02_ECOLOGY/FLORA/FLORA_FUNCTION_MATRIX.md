# Mythroot Prehistoric World — Flora Function Matrix v0.1

**Status:** Broad working framework. Not locked canon.

## Core principle

Do not begin by asking:

> "What plants should exist?"

Begin by asking:

> **"What ecological work needs to be done by the plant community?"**

Plants are not scenery. They are the foundation of the food web, climate feedbacks, soil formation, water systems, shelter, and human resources.

The eventual plant species should be invented to perform ecological functions appropriate to their climate and geography.

---

# I. PRIMARY ENERGY CAPTURE

The basic producers that turn stellar energy into usable biological energy.

Possible functions:
- terrestrial photosynthetic ground cover;
- low dense vegetation;
- shrubs;
- woody plants;
- aquatic algae/plant analogues;
- wetland producers;
- seasonal growth communities.

The exact photosynthetic biology remains open until the atmospheric model is finalized.

---

# II. STRUCTURAL VEGETATION

Plants that create the physical architecture of terrestrial habitats.

Possible functions:
- canopy;
- understory;
- dense thickets;
- low woodland;
- tall grass/grass analogues where climate permits;
- broad-leafed structural plants;
- woody stands;
- climbing/vining structures.

For this world, structural vegetation should generally favor **width, density, and robustness** over extreme vertical height, consistent with the established slightly heavier gravity and prehistoric visual target.

This does not mean every plant is short.

---

# III. GROUND COVER

Plants that occupy the lowest terrestrial layer.

Functions:
- prevent soil erosion;
- capture nutrients;
- provide food for small herbivores;
- provide nesting/shelter habitat;
- retain moisture;
- recolonize disturbed ground.

This is particularly important for creating the desired impression of **thick, lush ground-level vegetation**.

---

# IV. GRAZING RESOURCES

Plant communities specifically capable of supporting large herbivore biomass.

Possible functions:
- grasses/grass analogues;
- low herbaceous mats;
- fast-growing seasonal vegetation;
- tough perennial ground cover;
- nutrient-rich wetland plants.

The question is not "Do we have grass?"

The question is:

> **What plant community can support enormous grazing animals here?**

---

# V. BROWSE / LEAF RESOURCES

Plants that support browsers rather than grazers.

Possible functions:
- shrubs;
- low trees;
- dense leafy plants;
- woody shoots;
- broad-leafed vegetation;
- seasonal foliage.

Different plant structures should create different feeding niches.

---

# VI. FRUIT / SEED / NUT RESOURCES

Plants that move energy through specialized animal relationships.

Functions:
- seasonal food pulses;
- animal-mediated seed dispersal;
- long-distance plant migration;
- support for specialized consumers;
- creation of concentrated food resources.

These plants can become extremely important to prehistoric humans even if they are not dominant vegetation.

---

# VII. ROOT / TUBER / UNDERGROUND RESOURCES

Plants that store energy underground.

Functions:
- survive seasonal drought;
- survive fire/disturbance;
- provide concentrated food;
- stabilize soil;
- create food resources available even when surface vegetation is poor.

This is particularly useful for human survival because underground resources can provide reliable seasonal calories.

---

# VIII. WETLAND / RIPARIAN PLANTS

Plants adapted to lakes, rivers, marshes, floodplains, and shallow water.

Functions:
- stabilize banks;
- slow water;
- create nursery habitat;
- filter nutrients;
- provide food;
- create dense shelter.

These communities will be extremely important around the Rift Continent's rift lakes and inland seas.

---

# IX. AQUATIC PRODUCERS

Plants/algae/analogues that support aquatic food webs.

Functions:
- primary aquatic production;
- oxygenation;
- habitat;
- nutrient cycling;
- food for aquatic grazers and filter feeders.

Different water bodies may have very different producer communities.

---

# X. SOIL BUILDERS

Plants that change the soil rather than merely growing in it.

Functions:
- nitrogen fixation or an equivalent nutrient relationship;
- organic matter accumulation;
- mineral mobilization;
- soil stabilization;
- microbial symbiosis;
- creation of fertile patches.

These organisms help explain why productive ecosystems can exist on otherwise poor substrates.

---

# XI. DISTURBANCE COLONIZERS

Plants adapted to newly exposed or damaged terrain.

Possible triggers:
- volcanic eruptions;
- landslides;
- floods;
- fires;
- storms;
- exposed riverbanks;
- newly formed soils.

These are especially important on Rift.

A volcanic landscape does not remain barren forever.

---

# XII. FIRE / DISTURBANCE SPECIALISTS

If later climate modeling supports regular fire regimes, some plant lineages can evolve around them.

Possible strategies:
- fire-resistant underground structures;
- rapid post-fire growth;
- seeds triggered by heat;
- thick protective bark;
- fast colonization.

This category remains provisional until the regional climate/fire model is developed.

---

# XIII. CLIMBING / ATTACHMENT PLANTS

Plants that exploit existing structure.

Functions:
- use larger plants as support;
- create dense habitat;
- connect vertical layers;
- provide food and shelter.

These can be especially important in dense forests without requiring enormous trees.

---

# XIV. COASTAL / SHORELINE PLANTS

Plants adapted to:
- salt exposure;
- shifting sediment;
- flooding;
- tidal influence;
- coastal erosion.

Functions:
- stabilize shorelines;
- trap sediment;
- provide nursery habitat;
- create coastal shelter.

The exact marine/coastal plant chemistry remains open.

---

# XV. MEDICINAL / BIOACTIVE PLANTS

Plants with compounds that affect animal physiology.

This category is especially important to the setting's low-grade magic concept.

The default explanation remains biological/material rather than supernatural:

- plants contain active compounds;
- preparation changes availability or concentration;
- ash, bone, clay, oils, fats, water, and other materials can alter delivery;
- some preparations can reduce symptoms, promote recovery, reduce infection risk, or otherwise assist the body's natural processes.

The setting's spiritual component may interact with these practices, but plant biology remains the primary material foundation unless specific supernatural effects are later established.

---

# XVI. TOXIC / DEFENSIVE PLANTS

Plants that discourage consumption.

Possible strategies:
- toxins;
- irritants;
- bitter compounds;
- thorns/spines;
- difficult-to-digest tissues;
- chemical defenses.

These create selective pressures on herbivores and can become important sources of human knowledge.

---

# XVII. ANIMAL-DEPENDENT PLANTS

Plants that rely heavily on animals.

Possible relationships:
- pollination;
- seed dispersal;
- germination triggered by digestion;
- protection by animal partners;
- nutrient exchange.

This is where fauna and flora become a genuinely interconnected system rather than separate lists.

---

# XVIII. ECOSYSTEM ENGINEER PLANTS

Plants that physically reshape their environment.

Examples of functions:
- creating shade;
- altering water flow;
- trapping sediment;
- forming islands/mats;
- stabilizing slopes;
- creating dense barriers;
- changing soil chemistry;
- forming wetlands.

A plant can therefore influence an entire ecosystem without being the dominant food source.

---

# XIX. SUCCESSIONAL COMMUNITIES

Plant communities should change over time.

A disturbed landscape may progress through:

**bare substrate → pioneer community → grasses/herbaceous growth → shrub/thicket → woodland/forest or another stable community**

The exact sequence varies by climate.

This gives the world ecological history rather than static biomes.

---

# XX. REGIONAL SHARED FLORA

The three main continental systems should share broad ancestral plant functions and many related lineages.

### Hearth
More connected populations and large continuous ecological ranges.

### Shattered
Regional variants created by isolation between enormous fragments.

### Rift
Strong specialization around volcanic soils, rift lakes, wetlands, elevation, and disturbance.

### Lost
Deep independent evolution creates ancient surviving lineages, divergent descendants, and potentially entirely different solutions to familiar ecological functions.

---

# XXI. UNKNOWN FLORA CREATION RULES

Anyone creating a new plant should be able to answer:

### 1. What does it require?
Light, water, minerals, temperature, soil/substrate, elevation, etc.

### 2. What does it produce?
Leaves, fruit, seeds, roots, nectar, biomass, toxins, oils, fibers, etc.

### 3. Who uses it?
Herbivores, insects/arthropods, fungi/microbes, humans, aquatic organisms, etc.

### 4. How does it reproduce?
Seeds, spores, runners, fragmentation, animal dispersal, water dispersal, etc.

### 5. What limits it?
Drought, cold, herbivory, competition, fire, disease, soil chemistry, seasonal cycles, etc.

### 6. What does it change?
Soil, water, shade, sediment, habitat, nutrient availability, etc.

### 7. What happens if it disappears?
This identifies its ecological importance.

### 8. Why does it belong here?
Its anatomy and chemistry should follow the climate, geology, and ecological pressures of its region.

---

# XXII. FLORA DESIGN TEST

A proposed plant/community should survive five questions:

**Energy:** Where does its energy come from?

**Resources:** What does it require?

**Pressure:** What prevents it from dominating everything?

**Relationships:** What organisms depend on, consume, pollinate, disperse, or compete with it?

**History:** Why did this lineage evolve here?

If those answers work, the plant can be visually unfamiliar.

---

# XXIII. CURRENT HORIZONTAL FLORA CHECKLIST

Before designing individual species, every major continental system should eventually have broad representatives of:

- primary producers;
- structural vegetation;
- ground cover;
- grazing resources;
- browse resources;
- fruit/seed/root resources;
- wetland plants;
- aquatic producers;
- soil builders;
- disturbance colonizers;
- medicinal/bioactive plants;
- defensive/toxic plants;
- animal-dependent plants;
- ecosystem engineers.

The next step is to **overlay climate and geography onto these functions for Hearth, Shattered, Rift, and Lost**, then identify which plant functions are universal, regional, or unique.
