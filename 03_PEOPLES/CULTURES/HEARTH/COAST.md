# HEARTH — COASTAL PEOPLES
## Cultural Base v0.2 — Communal Labor & Food Preservation Integrated

**Status:** WORKING CANON / PEOPLES PHASE

## Core cultural philosophy

> **No single person can know what tomorrow will require.**

The Coastal peoples respond to an unpredictable but highly productive environment through versatility, redundancy, communal labor, and constant exchange.

Everyone learns enough of the community's major survival skills to remain useful when conditions change.

## Community structure

Typical communities are approximately **80–150 people**, with particularly productive coastal locations potentially supporting larger populations.

The village is simultaneously:
- home;
- school;
- workshop;
- food-processing center;
- fishing community;
- emergency-response unit.

## Generalist education

Children rotate through the community's major activities.

They learn:
- fishing;
- gathering;
- hunting;
- tracking;
- shoreline reading;
- guarding;
- toolmaking;
- animal handling;
- food processing;
- construction and repair;
- weaving/net work;
- watercraft/raft use where applicable.

A person may become exceptionally skilled in one discipline, but specialization is layered **on top of general competence** rather than replacing it.

A fishing expert still knows how to gather.
A toolmaker still knows how to fish.
A hunter still knows enough of the other community skills to remain useful.

## Communal labor waves

Coastal labor is organized primarily as **large community work waves**, rather than permanent occupational groups.

When a major task is needed, the community often performs it together.

Examples:

### Fishing period
Most able-bodied adults participate in fishing, while elders, very young children, injured people, and essential camp/watch roles remain behind as needed.

### Communal meal
The fishing group returns and the community shares a major meal, including elders and children.

### Craft/production period
The community may then shift collectively into:
- weaving;
- net repair;
- toolmaking;
- tanning;
- food processing;
- construction;
- maintenance;
- other current priorities.

### Exceptions
Not every person participates in every task.

Age, injury, pregnancy, disability, childcare, animal care, watch duties, and task-specific requirements can create smaller working groups.

The important distinction is that **the default organization is communal waves, not permanent occupational separation**.

## Why communal labor works

This system creates extreme redundancy.

If a skilled fisher dies, the community has not lost fishing knowledge.

If a toolmaker is injured, others retain basic toolmaking competence.

If several hunters are lost, the community still has people who can hunt.

Expertise remains valuable, but the community does not allow essential knowledge to become dependent on a handful of people.

## Social consequence

Work itself becomes a major social activity.

People learn by doing alongside one another.

Large tasks produce:
- teaching;
- joking;
- argument;
- cooperation;
- shared stories;
- competition;
- informal mentorship;
- communal pride.

The community does not merely gather after work.

**Much of the work is itself communal life.**

## Working cultural principle

> **No one carries the whole burden when everyone can carry part of it.**

## Coastal travel

The shoreline functions as a natural travel corridor.

Coastal people maintain knowledge of:
- coves;
- beaches;
- headlands;
- freshwater outlets;
- tidal flats;
- safe crossings;
- dangerous rocks;
- seasonal fish runs;
- nesting areas;
- storm shelters.

This creates a continuous cultural corridor between communities.

## Tide knowledge

The Coastal peoples treat tides as practical scheduling information.

Children learn to recognize:
- when shellfish beds are exposed;
- when channels become dangerous;
- when boats/rafts can launch;
- when particular fish appear;
- when shoreline predators are active.

The moon's cycles become an observational calendar because people repeatedly associate lunar patterns with changing water behavior.

## Storm response

Storms are a community problem, not the responsibility of a permanent specialist caste.

People know:
- when to leave the water;
- where vulnerable people go;
- how to secure shelters;
- how to protect food and equipment;
- how to reorganize labor rapidly.

General competence makes the village resilient.

## Food preservation

The coast provides unusually convenient access to salt.

Coastal communities learn that concentrated seawater can leave salt deposits and that salt can preserve fish and other foods.

They develop deliberate small-scale salt collection/evaporation practices using locally available surfaces and containers.

Salt becomes a major preservation technology alongside drying and other food-preservation methods.

This gives Coastal communities an important form of food security:
**a successful fishing season can be turned into stored food rather than requiring immediate consumption.**

It can support somewhat larger and more stable populations where ecological productivity permits.

## Cuisine

Most Hearth peoples have limited access to dedicated spices.

Typical food is based on:
- meat;
- fish;
- grains/seeds;
- roots;
- greens;
- fruit;
- nuts;
- eggs;
- honey;
- locally available edible plants.

The Coastal people have an unusually reliable flavoring and preservation resource in **salt**.

Honey remains an important sweetener/flavoring resource.

Different regions may possess additional flavorful plants, but a developed spice culture is not assumed.

## Coastal hazards

Coastal life is not simply safer because food is abundant.

Major hazards include:
- storms;
- tides;
- drowning;
- unstable shorelines;
- marine predators;
- venomous aquatic creatures;
- giant marine animals;
- stranding;
- navigation failures;
- contaminated or unavailable freshwater.

Their advantage is resilience and preservation, not immunity from danger.

## River relationship

Coastal and River peoples share an ancient aquatic cultural relationship.

Possible shared elements include:
- ancient vocabulary;
- water traditions;
- stories of ancestral movement;
- related technologies;
- overlapping ecological knowledge.

Neither culture necessarily knows which came first.

River communities emphasize **flow**.

Coastal communities emphasize **the edge**.

Boundary communities may be culturally difficult to classify as purely River or Coastal.

## Gatherings

Coastal communities can voluntarily converge around major productive locations such as:
- seasonal fish runs;
- spawning grounds;
- shellfish concentrations;
- calm-water coves;
- productive seasonal plant resources.

Gatherings support:
- trade;
- marriage;
- games;
- storytelling;
- knowledge exchange;
- courtship;
- communal fishing;
- repairs;
- environmental information exchange.

Unlike Wetland gatherings, these are not necessarily forced by environmental displacement.

## Shared cultural identity

The coastline itself connects communities.

People, stories, technologies, tools, marriage relationships, and information move along the shore.

A traveler arriving from another coastal community is therefore more likely to be recognized as part of a connected cultural world.

The shoreline functions as both:
- **road**
- **information network**
