**Document class:** ANALYTICAL REFERENCE — WORKING DESIGN
**Purpose:** Organize source-derived Hearth material on elders, aging, care, knowledge succession, dying, death, and continuing kin obligations before final spiritual/death customs are designed.
**Authority:** Regional cultural documents remain authoritative. This document does not promote open questions to canon.

The Hearth Cultural Audit identifies Elders / Death as:

- **Plains:** PART
- **Mountains:** PART

## 2. SHARED HEARTH PRINCIPLE

Across the existing material, age does not automatically remove a person's social value.

Aging changes **what a person contributes**, especially when physical mobility or strength declines. Knowledge, memory, teaching, judgment, childcare, craft, coordination, and accumulated environmental understanding can become increasingly important forms of contribution.

This fits the broader Hearth principle that survival depends on knowledge, cooperation, adaptation, and dependable contribution.

This is a comparative principle, not a claim that all six cultures organize elderhood identically.

---

# 3. PLAINS

### Existing foundation
Plains society combines mobile groups with recurring anchor communities. People whose bodies can no longer support continuous travel can remain at anchor communities rather than being removed from the social system.

### Elder role
Elders naturally become more important as:

- teachers of accumulated knowledge;
- keepers of routes, stories, and community memory;
- childcare and household support;
- craft and maintenance workers where mobility is less important;
- advisors and coordinators;
- links between mobile people and anchor communities.

### Aging
Aging is therefore likely to produce a **change of role rather than social disappearance**. The transition from mobile labor to anchor-community contribution should be treated as an established structural possibility.

### Death — open
The current source material does not establish:

- burial/disposal method;
- mourning customs;
- memorial practice;
- ancestor beliefs;
- afterlife;
- treatment of the dying;
- community response to catastrophic death.

These remain open.

---

# 4. MOUNTAINS

### Existing foundation
Mountain family structure is organized around extended specialist households/lineages. The family is simultaneously household, teaching institution, and repository of specialist knowledge.

The Knowledge & Care House explicitly includes elder knowledge, history, oral tradition, medicine, birth, child development, and cultural-memory preservation.

### Elder role
Elders therefore have unusually strong structural value as:

- repositories of specialist knowledge;
- teachers;
- historians and oral tradition keepers;
- advisors to the household/lineage;
- transmitters of techniques and standards;
- participants in care and community continuity.

### Aging
Physical decline does not necessarily end specialist value. A person may transition from physically demanding work toward teaching, oversight, memory preservation, and judgment.

### Death — open
The source does not yet establish whether specialist knowledge is believed to continue after death, whether ancestors are spiritually involved, or how bodies are treated.

These questions must remain separate from the established fact that elders preserve knowledge while alive.

---

# 5. RIVER

### Existing foundation
River kinship extends across a connected geographic corridor, with bilateral family ties and broader river kin relationships. This gives the River people a strong existing network through which responsibilities can extend upstream and downstream.

### Elder role — development direction
The logical design question is not simply "what do elders do?" but:

> How does a society whose family network follows the river distribute care and accumulated knowledge when people age?

Potential source-compatible areas for development include:

- upstream/downstream knowledge transmission;
- navigation and seasonal memory;
- kin mediation;
- childcare across extended family networks;
- teaching river ecology and hazards;
- hosting/supporting river kin.

These are **design questions**, not canon.

### Death — open
No final River death custom, mourning system, burial/disposal practice, ancestor belief, or afterlife is established in the current reference material.

---

# 6. WETLANDS

### Existing foundation
The Wetlands use a 12–15-person functional group as the primary daily household/survival unit inside a cooperative village. Work exchanges and seasonal cooperation broaden the teaching network beyond the immediate group.

### Elder role — development direction
Aging should be examined through the relationship between:

- functional group;
- village;
- seasonal labor;
- ecological memory;
- flood knowledge;
- childcare;
- specialized practical knowledge.

An elder who cannot perform the same physical work may still contribute through accumulated knowledge, teaching, observation, preparation, childcare, and coordination.

The exact institutional form remains open.

### Death — open
No final Wetlands death custom, burial/disposal practice, mourning system, memorial tradition, ancestor concept, or afterlife is established.

---

# 7. DESERT

### Existing foundation
Desert communities are connected through oases and the Lifekeeper network. Lifekeepers carry knowledge about water, routes, animals, weather, and distant conditions. Elders already have an established educational role in accumulated environmental and survival knowledge.

### Elder role
Elders can therefore function as:

- repositories of environmental memory;
- teachers of survival judgment;
- interpreters of long-term changes in routes, weather, and water conditions;
- advisors to families and oasis communities;
- mentors to younger specialists and prospective Lifekeepers.

### Aging
As physical travel becomes harder, accumulated knowledge can become more important, while highly mobile duties may pass to younger people.

### Death — partially open
The audit marks this layer PART rather than GAP, indicating some elder/death-related material exists, but the current comparative reference does not establish a complete funerary system. Final burial, mourning, memorial, ancestor, and afterlife decisions remain open unless a regional source establishes them explicitly.

---

# 8. COAST

### Existing foundation
Coastal society is highly communal. Community functions as the practical household, and biological parentage is recognized without making parents the only adults responsible for children.

Elders remain within communal life even when they cannot participate in the same level of physical labor.

### Elder role — development direction
The key cultural question is how a communal household treats accumulated knowledge when physical contribution declines.

Likely areas to investigate from existing culture include:

- teaching;
- childcare;
- food knowledge and preservation;
- weather and shoreline memory;
- navigation knowledge;
- communal history;
- mediation and social memory.

These remain development questions until supported by regional source material.

### Death — open
No complete coastal funerary, mourning, memorial, ancestor, or afterlife system is currently established.

---

# 9. AGING AS A SOCIAL TRANSITION

The current material supports a useful cross-cultural distinction:

**Aging is not necessarily a transition from useful → useless.**

Instead, each culture should answer:

1. What physical work becomes difficult with age?
2. What knowledge becomes more valuable with age?
3. Who cares for someone whose mobility declines?
4. Does the elder remain in the same household/group?
5. Can elders relocate to a safer or more accessible part of the community?
6. What authority, if any, accompanies age?
7. How is knowledge transferred before death?
8. What happens when an elder dies far from home?

The answers should differ according to ecology and social structure.

---

# 10. KNOWLEDGE SUCCESSION

This is especially important because Hearth culture treats knowledge as a survival resource.

The design problem is therefore not simply "what happens to old people?"

It is:

> **How does a society prevent accumulated knowledge from dying with the person who carries it?**

Potential mechanisms to evaluate later include:

- apprenticeship;
- oral history;
- repeated teaching;
- family/lineage instruction;
- community archives or objects;
- route memorization;
- specialist houses;
- elder councils or advisory roles;
- ritualized knowledge transfer.

These mechanisms are not automatically canon.

---

# 11. CARE OF THE DYING

The current Hearth references do not establish a universal answer.

Before designing regional death customs, determine whether each culture emphasizes:

- family presence;
- community presence;
- specialist/healer assistance;
- practical comfort;
- solitude;
- continued work until incapacity;
- preparation of the body;
- spoken farewells;
- transfer of knowledge or responsibilities before death.

Because Aruun has subtle real magic, treatment of the dying must also preserve the setting's established distinction: ritual or medicine may genuinely help, stabilize, or improve the chance of recovery, but cannot guarantee an outcome.

---

# 12. DEATH CUSTOMS — DELIBERATELY UNRESOLVED

Across all six peoples, do not finalize the following until the spiritual foundation is developed:

- burial;
- cremation;
- exposure;
- water disposal;
- preservation;
- body return to ecosystem;
- grave marking;
- mourning duration;
- memorials;
- inheritance immediately after death;
- ancestor concepts;
- communication with the dead;
- afterlife;
- reincarnation;
- spirits of the dead;
- treatment of violent or accidental death;
- treatment of children who die;
- catastrophic-death response.

The absence of an answer is intentional.

---

# 13. CATASTROPHIC DEATH

This should eventually be treated as a separate cultural institution from ordinary death.

Each culture may need different mechanisms for responding to:

- flood;
- drought;
- storm;
- avalanche;
- wildfire;
- epidemic;
- travel disaster;
- warfare;
- predator attack;
- community destruction.

The response should follow each culture's existing social structure rather than using one universal Hearth funeral model.

---

# 14. CROSS-CULTURAL DESIGN QUESTIONS

Before finalizing regional death customs, compare:

### Mobility
How does a culture handle death when the person dies away from their normal community?

### Kinship
Who has responsibility for the dying and dead?

### Knowledge
What happens to specialist knowledge held by the deceased?

### Ecology
Does the environment make certain forms of body disposal practical or impractical?

### Community structure
Does death primarily affect a household, lineage, functional group, village, oasis, mobile band, or entire communal settlement?

### Spirituality
Does the culture understand death as an ending, transformation, return, continuation, separation, or something else?

The final answer to the last question belongs in the belief/worldview layer.
