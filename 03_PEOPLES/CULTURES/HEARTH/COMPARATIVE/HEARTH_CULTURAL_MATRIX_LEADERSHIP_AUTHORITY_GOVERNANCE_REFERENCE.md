## 1. SHARED HEARTH PATTERN

Across the six Hearth peoples, authority is generally tied to **competence, trust, responsibility, or specialized knowledge** rather than a single continent-wide sovereign structure.

The existing Hearth canon describes regional governance as culturally specific. There is no source-supported basis here for inventing a universal Hearth government.

The recurring question is therefore not simply **“Who rules?”** but:

> **Who is trusted to make a particular kind of decision, and why?**

This produces different regional answers.

# 3. PLAINS

## Authority

Plains authority is **distributed and role-based**.

Hunt leaders, scout leaders, craft leaders, migration leaders, and resource leaders hold authority appropriate to the immediate problem. Trust is explicitly treated as the central currency of authority.

A person's authority therefore comes from being trusted to handle a responsibility, not from permanently ruling everyone.

## Decision pattern

Authority can move with the task:

**problem → most trusted competent person → temporary responsibility → accountability**

## Conflict

Disputes begin within the relevant work group. Possible consequences include:
- restitution;
- temporary loss of authority;
- reassignment;
- removal from dangerous responsibility;
- loss of trust;
- extreme exclusion.

## Cultural implication

Plains governance fits the mobile structure: permanent centralized rulers would be poorly matched to a society organized through households, mobile groups, anchor communities, and a broad cultural network.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED in current Hearth canon.

---

# 4. MOUNTAINS

## Authority

Mountain authority is strongly tied to **specialist houses and lineage heads**.

The family is simultaneously household, teaching institution, and repository of specialist knowledge. House heads coordinate:
- training;
- specialist knowledge;
- relevant resources;
- marriages;
- internal disputes;
- regional representation.

## Village and regional coordination

Village coordination occurs through house heads.

The **Mountain Conclave** provides regional coordination and supports:
- marriage exchange;
- specialist population balancing;
- knowledge exchange;
- trade;
- relationship maintenance;
- regional problems.

## Cultural implication

Mountain governance is the strongest example of **institutionalized specialized authority** among the six peoples.

It is not simply hereditary political nobility: authority is connected to the preservation and management of specialist competence.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED foundation in current Hearth canon.

---

# 5. RIVER

## Authority

River authority is **situational and knowledge-centered**.

The existing canon describes strong elder and knowledge-holder influence while avoiding centralized river-wide government.

Reputation is particularly important because relationships extend upstream and downstream.

## Decision pattern

The river network encourages authority based on:
- practical knowledge;
- experience;
- reputation;
- ability to navigate relationships between communities.

## Conflict

River justice emphasizes restitution and restoration of relationships.

Because reputation travels through the river corridor, social consequences can extend well beyond the immediate settlement.

## Cultural implication

The river itself acts as a social network, so governance is distributed across connected communities rather than concentrated in one capital or ruler.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED at the broad structural level; exact institutions remain less developed.

---

# 6. WETLANDS

## Authority

Wetland leadership is described as **temporary, functional, and task-based**.

The person most trusted for the current problem coordinates that activity:
- experienced hunter during a dangerous hunt;
- experienced builder during construction;
- best flood reader during seasonal relocation;
- skilled gatherer during difficult harvest.

The same person does not automatically lead every activity.

## Inter-group governance

Inter-group decisions are coordinated by representatives of affected groups rather than permanent rulers.

The 12–15-person functional group remains the primary daily unit, while the village exists as a cooperative system.

## Conflict

Restoration is preferred. Consequences can include:
- repayment;
- replacement labor;
- temporary reassignment/separation;
- loss of trust;
- removal from responsibilities;
- permanent exclusion in extreme repeated cases that threaten communal survival.

## Cultural implication

Wetlands governance reflects **distributed competence** and the communal-separatist structure.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad structure; several exact mechanics remain working proposals.

---

# 7. DESERT

## Authority

Desert society separates different forms of specialized authority.

### Elders
Elders guide family and community decisions.

### Water Watchers
Water Watchers hold specialized authority over sustainable water **use**. They:
- monitor water quality and levels;
- maintain collection points;
- protect drinking areas;
- maintain emergency reserves;
- coordinate rationing during scarcity.

They do not own the water or become absolute rulers.

### Lifekeepers
Lifekeepers possess specialized authority concerning:
- routes;
- environmental danger;
- travel;
- distant water conditions;
- animal movements;
- weather;
- survival information.

Their status is based on demonstrated competence and training rather than a hereditary ruling caste.

## Coordination

Water Watchers and Lifekeepers exchange information. Neither appears to control the entire desert network.

## Cultural implication

Desert governance is divided according to **survival responsibilities**:

**local stewardship + mobile environmental knowledge + elder guidance**.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad structure; exact inter-oasis institutions remain partly open.

---

# 8. COAST

## Authority

Coastal authority is **communal and competence-based**.

Task coordinators organize activities such as:
- fishing;
- construction;
- food preservation;
- defense;
- child learning;
- other communal work.

They do not become permanent rulers merely because they coordinate a task.

## Major decisions

Major decisions seek broad communal agreement.

Elders have substantial advisory influence because of accumulated knowledge and experience.

## Conflict

Serious disputes are brought before respected adults/elders. Consequences emphasize:
- apology;
- restitution;
- repair work;
- temporary removal from responsibilities;
- separation where necessary;
- communal loss of trust.

## Cultural implication

Coastal governance follows directly from the communal household model: the community itself is the primary social unit, so authority is expected to remain embedded within the community rather than becoming a separate ruling class.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad structure; exact decision-making procedures remain less developed.

---

# 9. CROSS-REGIONAL COMPARISON

The six systems can be summarized without treating them as interchangeable:

### Plains
**Trust the person who can handle the task.**

### Mountains
**Trust the house that preserves the knowledge.**

### River
**Trust experience, reputation, and knowledge of the network.**

### Wetlands
**Let the most capable person lead the current task.**

### Desert
**Give specialized responsibility to those trained to protect survival resources.**

### Coast
**Let the community coordinate itself through trusted competent people.**

These are analytical summaries, not new canon statements.

---

# 10. WHAT IS ALREADY SOLID

The existing Hearth material gives us a strong foundation for:

1. authority being regional rather than continent-wide;
2. competence and trust being major sources of legitimacy;
3. specialist authority being strongest in Mountains;
4. role-based authority being strongest in Plains and Wetlands;
5. specialized survival offices being important in Desert;
6. reputation/network knowledge being important in River;
7. communal coordination being central to Coast;
8. permanent universal monarchy or continent-wide government not being established by these sources.

# 11. OPEN DEVELOPMENT QUESTIONS

These should remain **OPEN**, not invented here:

1. What exactly qualifies someone to become an elder in each culture?
2. How are leaders selected or recognized when multiple competent people disagree?
3. What happens when regional customs conflict with one another?
4. What institutions handle disputes between communities rather than individuals?
5. How are children formally taught governance and responsibility?
6. How are decisions recorded, remembered, or transmitted?
7. What authority exists during continental-scale emergencies?
8. What happens when a specialist authority abuses its responsibility?
9. How do Plains, River, Wetlands, and Coast communities coordinate beyond their normal local networks?
10. What formal institutions, if any, exist above the village/community level?

No answer should be promoted into canon until supported by existing material or deliberately chosen as a new setting decision.
