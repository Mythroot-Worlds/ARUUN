**Status:** ANALYTICAL REFERENCE — NOT CANON

## 1. SHARED PATTERN

The current Hearth material consistently treats work as part of survival and social life rather than as a detached economic system.

The regional systems differ because their environments and social structures make different kinds of knowledge and labor especially important.

A useful analytical question is:

> **What does this people need to produce, preserve, exchange, or know in order for its community to remain resilient?**

# 3. PLAINS

## Work organization

Plains society separates daily life into overlapping households, mobile work/travel groups, recurring anchor communities, and a broader cultural network.

Mobile groups commonly contain roughly 10–25 people, with 10–15 common. They are flexible rather than permanently familial.

Movement follows:
- herbivore migrations;
- seed and grass cycles;
- fruiting seasons;
- reliable water;
- floodplain productivity.

## Work

The existing canon identifies role-based leadership for hunting, scouting, crafting, migration, and resource management. Children progressively enter these activities as they become capable.

The economy therefore appears highly **seasonal, mobile, and skill-sharing**, rather than centered on permanent occupational institutions.

## Anchor communities

Anchor communities provide recurring places for:
- elders who can no longer travel;
- very young children;
- injured people;
- craft workers;
- teachers;
- stored resources.

They therefore function as part of the economic system without replacing mobility.

## Exchange

The broader Plains cultural network shares:
- seasonal routes;
- marriage networks;
- hunting knowledge;
- plant knowledge;
- meeting places;
- stories;
- language continuums.

Exact formal trade mechanisms remain underdeveloped in the source material.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad economic structure; detailed trade law/currency remains open.

# 4. MOUNTAINS

## Work organization

Mountain work is strongly organized through **specialist houses/lineages**.

The house functions simultaneously as:
- family structure;
- teaching institution;
- specialist knowledge repository;
- resource coordination unit.

The current Mountain update explicitly states that houses should maintain **multiple related competencies**, not necessarily one narrow occupation.

## Production and specialization

Specialist competence is highly valued because knowledge must survive across generations.

House heads coordinate:
- training;
- specialist population;
- relevant resources;
- specialist knowledge;
- marriages;
- representation.

## Exchange

The Mountain Conclave coordinates:
- trade;
- knowledge exchange;
- marriage exchange;
- specialist population balancing;
- major regional problems.

The exact house count, specialty bundles, and trade mechanisms remain open.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED structure; detailed economic institutions remain open.

# 5. RIVER

## Work organization

River society is organized around extended households embedded in a larger river-corridor network.

Practical competence centers on understanding and moving through the river environment.

Children learn:
- fishing;
- gathering;
- swimming;
- currents;
- plants;
- animals;
- boats/rafts;
- weather.

Adolescents accompany experienced adults on supervised journeys to other river settlements.

## Network economy

River communities are connected upstream and downstream. The same corridor that carries people and information also supports exchange.

The existing material supports:
- river-connected hospitality;
- resource information exchange;
- travel knowledge;
- goods/labor relationships;
- trade relationships extending beyond one settlement.

## Reputation

Because reputation travels along the river, reliable exchange is reinforced socially. Repeated dishonesty can damage a person's ability to receive hospitality or trade.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad network economy; exact market/trade institutions remain open.

# 6. WETLANDS

## Work organization

The primary daily economic unit is a **12–15-person functional group**.

Groups can include:
- hunters;
- gatherers;
- fishers;
- weavers;
- toolmakers;
- animal handlers;
- scouts/watchers;
- food processors.

## Exchange

Groups are substantially self-contained but not isolated.

They:
- share surplus;
- lend labor;
- compensate for failed hunts or gathering;
- assist with major tasks;
- support one another after injury or loss.

This creates a distributed village economy without requiring a permanent centralized authority.

## Children and labor

Children rotate through multiple practical skills. Adolescents can spend seasons with other groups, which spreads both knowledge and labor capacity.

## Cultural implication

Wetlands economic resilience comes from **small-group independence combined with inter-group redundancy**.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad structure; exact exchange rules remain open.

# 7. DESERT

## Work organization

Desert society is centered on extended family households within oasis communities, connected through the larger **Lifekeeper network**.

## Lifekeepers

Lifekeepers are trained mobile specialists responsible for:
- movement;
- environmental knowledge;
- hunting;
- route knowledge;
- distant conditions;
- water/travel information;
- connecting oases.

Their status is based on demonstrated competence rather than a hereditary ruling caste.

## Water Watchers

Water Watchers protect the usability and sustainability of oasis water by:
- checking water quality;
- monitoring levels;
- maintaining collection points;
- protecting drinking areas from contamination;
- maintaining emergency reserves;
- coordinating rationing during scarcity.

They regulate **use**, not ownership.

## Exchange

The Lifekeeper network naturally provides an information and travel corridor between oases. The existing material supports exchange of environmental information, travel knowledge, and resources between connected communities.

Exact formal trade law, currency, and market structure remain open.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad economic structure; detailed trade system remains open.

# 8. COAST

## Work organization

Coastal communities organize labor primarily through **large communal work waves**, rather than permanent occupational groups.

A major task can bring much of the community together.

Examples include:
- fishing periods;
- communal meals;
- weaving;
- net repair;
- toolmaking;
- tanning;
- food processing;
- construction;
- maintenance.

Exceptions arise from age, injury, pregnancy, disability, childcare, animal care, watch duties, and task-specific needs.

## General competence

Children rotate through:
- fishing;
- gathering;
- hunting;
- tracking;
- shoreline reading;
- guarding;
- toolmaking;
- animal handling;
- food processing;
- construction/repair;
- weaving/net work;
- watercraft/raft use where applicable.

Specialization exists, but it is layered **on top of general competence**.

## Preservation

Salt is a major Coastal preservation technology because concentrated seawater can leave salt deposits. Communities also use drying and other preservation methods.

This allows successful fishing seasons to become stored food rather than requiring immediate consumption.

## Exchange corridor

The shoreline functions as both:
- a travel road;
- an information network.

Communities can converge at seasonal productive locations for:
- trade;
- marriage;
- games;
- storytelling;
- knowledge exchange;
- courtship;
- communal fishing;
- repairs;
- environmental information exchange.

## Economic resilience

The Coastal system emphasizes **redundancy and communal labor**. Essential knowledge is not supposed to depend on a tiny specialist caste.

**Status:** ESTABLISHED broad structure; exact trade law and formal exchange mechanisms remain open.

|---|---|
| Plains | mobility + flexible labor + seasonal knowledge |
| Mountains | preservation of specialist knowledge |
| River | connected corridor + network relationships |
| Wetlands | small-group independence + surplus/labor sharing |
| Desert | water stewardship + mobile environmental specialists |
| Coast | general competence + communal labor + preservation |

These are analytical summaries, not new canon.

# 10. WHAT IS ALREADY SOLID

The source material establishes strong regional differences in how labor and resources are organized:

1. **Plains:** mobility and seasonal work are fundamental.
2. **Mountains:** specialist houses preserve knowledge and coordinate specialized production.
3. **River:** the river corridor functions as a social, informational, and economic network.
4. **Wetlands:** functional groups share surplus and labor while remaining substantially self-contained.
5. **Desert:** water stewardship and the Lifekeeper network are economically critical.
6. **Coast:** communal labor waves, general competence, salt, and preservation create strong redundancy.
