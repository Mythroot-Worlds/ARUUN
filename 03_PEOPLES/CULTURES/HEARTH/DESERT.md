# HEARTH — DESERT PEOPLES
## Cultural Base v0.3 — Lifekeeper Network & Trials Integrated

**Status:** WORKING CANON / PEOPLES PHASE

## Core philosophy

The Desert peoples resemble the Mountain peoples in some social pressures, but their isolation comes from **distance and water scarcity** rather than terrain.

> **Survival belongs to the people who know where to go, when to go, and what not to reveal.**

They are self-reliant, internally cohesive, cautious with outsiders, and exceptionally knowledgeable about water and routes.

## Environmental foundation

Sunscour is a major closed/endorheic basin with a network of perennial and seasonal water, springs, groundwater-fed oases, and a saline/playa-dominated deep basin.

The current working oasis model has five major perennial systems, secondary spring/groundwater systems, and numerous seasonal/emergency water sites.

## Community structure

Major oases can support roughly **50–120 people** where water and food productivity justify it. Other communities may be smaller or seasonal.

The Desert population is concentrated around the water network rather than evenly distributed across Sunscour.

## Two complementary lifestyles

### Oasis communities
Relatively stationary or semi-stationary. They provide stable food, storage, crafts, animal care, children/elder support, and refuge.

### Traveling hunters / Lifekeepers
Highly mobile. They hunt across the spaces between oases and distribute food, information, route knowledge, and environmental intelligence among communities.

The Desert peoples are therefore neither purely nomadic nor purely oasis-bound. They are a **network culture of water communities connected by mobile Lifekeepers and other travelers**.

## Lifekeepers

Lifekeepers are respected traveling hunters drawn from across the Desert population.

They are **not a hereditary caste** by default.

A candidate may come from any oasis or family if they demonstrate the necessary potential and are willing to learn.

They are expected to develop:
- strength;
- endurance;
- navigation;
- environmental awareness;
- judgment;
- teamwork;
- self-control;
- hunting competence;
- ability to protect themselves and others.

Lifekeepers rotate through multiple oasis communities rather than belonging exclusively to one village.

They provide:
- meat and other wild resources;
- information about distant regions;
- animal and environmental observations;
- warnings about changing water conditions;
- connections between communities;
- practical assistance when local resources fail.

Oasis communities provide Lifekeepers with:
- water;
- food when available;
- shelter;
- repairs;
- equipment;
- animal support;
- places to recover.

The relationship is reciprocal.

Lifekeepers are highly respected because they leave the relative safety of the oasis to help keep everyone else alive.

Their prestige does **not** make them a ruling class.

A Lifekeeper is understood as **of the people and for the people**.

## Recruitment

There is no single bloodline requirement.

A person must demonstrate the ability to hold their own in the desert and must be willing to learn.

Experienced Lifekeepers teach prospective hunters.

A child of famous hunters can fail to qualify.

A child from an unremarkable family can become an exceptional Lifekeeper.

The community cares more about demonstrated competence, judgment, endurance, and service than ancestry.

## Lifekeeper Trials

The Desert peoples possess a formal, recurring martial and survival qualification tradition known provisionally as the **Lifekeeper Trials**.

The Trials are a qualification for service rather than a contest for social dominance.

They bring prospective hunters, experienced Lifekeepers, families, trainers, elders, and people from multiple oases together.

### Physical trial

Candidates demonstrate practical desert fitness through tasks such as:
- long-distance travel;
- carrying substantial loads;
- climbing;
- difficult-terrain movement;
- maintaining pace without wasting water;
- endurance;
- returning to a designated location;
- basic first aid;
- equipment handling.

The standard is not simply raw strength.

The question is:

> **Can this person keep themselves and another person alive?**

A strong candidate who wastes water or becomes lost can fail.

### Combat trial

Candidates participate in controlled martial contests.

The purpose is to evaluate:
- reaction speed;
- footwork;
- grappling;
- controlled strikes;
- weapon handling;
- self-protection;
- disengagement;
- composure.

Winning a fight is not sufficient.

Candidates are judged on **control and judgment**.

A candidate who becomes reckless or loses control is demonstrating a dangerous weakness.

The ideal Lifekeeper can fight when necessary without needing to prove that they can fight.

### Judgment trial

Candidates are presented with difficult survival scenarios, potentially including:
- an injured companion;
- limited water;
- a distant oasis;
- a newly discovered animal herd;
- changing weather;
- conflicting priorities.

Evaluators watch how the candidate reasons, communicates, weighs risk, and protects the group.

The purpose is not necessarily to identify one universally correct answer.

The purpose is to determine whether the candidate can make sound decisions under pressure.

## Social function of the Trials

The Lifekeeper Trials also become a regional gathering.

They provide opportunities for:
- training;
- knowledge exchange;
- storytelling;
- trade;
- family meetings;
- courtship;
- games;
- friendly competition;
- celebration.

Serious qualification and ordinary human social life coexist.

The Trials are therefore both a survival institution and a cultural gathering.

## Isolation

Desert isolation is logistical.

A nearby community can be effectively distant when crossing the terrain requires scarce water and precise timing.

The oases are not necessarily socially hostile to one another. The mobile Lifekeepers are one of the major mechanisms that keeps the entire Desert population socially connected.

### Mountain comparison

**Mountain isolation:** communities are separated by terrain but deliberately reconnect through marriage, kinship, and inter-village exchange.

**Desert isolation:** the environment separates the population physically, while traveling Lifekeepers and other mobile people actively connect the oasis communities.

## Knowledge

Important knowledge includes:
- water locations;
- seasonal springs;
- groundwater signs;
- storm patterns;
- safe routes;
- shelter;
- edible plants;
- animal movement;
- dangerous terrain.

Knowledge may become concentrated in families or trusted groups, echoing the Mountain lineage model, but exact hereditary specialization remains open.

## Hospitality

A peaceful traveler may receive water, food, shelter, and immediate survival assistance.

But assistance does not automatically include revealing:
- hidden water sources;
- routes;
- caches;
- strategically important information.

Working distinction:

> **You may be kept alive without being given access to everything we know.**

## Daily rhythm

Desert activity follows temperature and light.

### Before dawn / early morning
Major travel, hunting, gathering, scouting, water movement, and animal work begin.

### Midday
Outdoor activity decreases sharply. People shelter in shade, rock shelters, caves, or constructed shade spaces.

This becomes a major period for eating, craft work, repair, teaching, planning, information exchange, and rest.

### Late afternoon / evening
Outdoor activity resumes. Travelers return, hunters report, resources are processed, animals are tended, and groups prepare for night.

### Night
Cooler temperatures make travel and some hunting attractive. The stars become a practical navigation reference.

## The desert has a memory

The people preserve detailed knowledge of old camps, shade, emergency water, seasonal springs, plant locations, safe routes, dangerous terrain, and animal watering sites.

Children learn places as well as techniques.

> **The desert is not owned by the people. It is remembered by them.**
