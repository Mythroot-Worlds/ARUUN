# MYTHROOT WORLDS — HEARTH PEOPLES
## Family, Partnership & Kinship Profiles — Working Draft v0.1

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT — REVIEW BEFORE LOCK

This document fills the family, household, partnership, and kinship gaps using the cultural systems established for Hearth. It distinguishes established decisions from working extrapolations.

---

# 1. MOUNTAINS — LOCKED FOUNDATION

## Social unit
The extended specialist household/lineage is the core unit.

The family is simultaneously household, teaching institution, and repository of specialist knowledge.

## Partner selection
Mountain people generally marry within the same specialist occupation/lineage:
- hunter ↔ hunter
- weaver ↔ weaver
- toolmaker ↔ toolmaker
- animal-handler ↔ animal-handler

The purpose is to preserve and strengthen specialized knowledge across generations.

Marriage is primarily a survival institution rather than a system based on romantic attraction.

## Approval
Heads of the relevant specialist houses arrange partnerships.

They consider:
- marriageable-age people;
- current specialist population;
- existing family connections;
- which village needs the specialists more.

## Annual Mountain Conclave
Mountain communities periodically gather in a major conclave to exchange marriageable partners and coordinate specialist populations.

The conclave also supports trade, knowledge exchange, relationship maintenance, and other regional business.

Remote communities may participate through regional gatherings or representatives when distance prevents attendance.

## Residence
A married couple normally stays with the specialist group in the village where their shared skill is most needed.

Example:
- Village A has 8 hunters.
- Village B has 6 hunters.
- A and B are participating in the current exchange.
- The hunter couple remains in B because B needs hunters more.

## Avoiding close kin marriage
The people do not need a scientific understanding of genetics.

They can possess accumulated practical knowledge that excessively close-family marriage produces bad outcomes.

Marriage exchanges therefore cycle among villages and families rather than repeatedly pairing the same close groups.

## Children
Children grow up within the extended specialist lineage and learn from many adults possessing the same skill.

A hunter child can therefore have a large network of hunting teachers across the family.

# 2. RIVER — WORKING PROFILE

## Social unit
Extended households of roughly 10–20 people:
- parents;
- children;
- grandparents;
- siblings;
- cousins;
- close kin.

The household is important, but the river corridor is the larger social network.

## Partner selection
Individuals have meaningful input into partnerships.

Families and communities help identify appropriate partners based on:
- age;
- health;
- kinship;
- family relationships;
- community needs;
- existing river connections.

Both prospective partners can refuse.

## Marriage network
Marriage follows the river corridor.

Communities maintain recurring relationships with settlements upstream and downstream.

Occupation does not determine marriage.

## Residence
Couples may:
- remain in one partner's community;
- move to the other partner's community;
- establish a new household where practical.

Moving does not sever the person's relationship with their original family.

## Kinship
River kinship is bilateral.

Children belong to both parents' families.

Family relationships can extend many settlements upstream and downstream.

Genealogical memory is practical knowledge: people remember who is related to whom and where those relatives live.

## River kin
A broader category of reciprocal relationship exists between communities with long-standing connections.

A traveler from a known river-connected community may be treated as river kin even without close biological relationship.

This creates a broad hospitality and mutual-assistance network.

## Separation
Partnerships can end without destroying the larger kin network.

Children remain connected to both sides.

Extended family and community help redistribute responsibilities.

## Cultural principle
> **The river has many bends, but it remains one river.**

# 3. WETLANDS — WORKING PROFILE

## Social unit
The 12–15-person functional group is the primary daily unit.

Each group is substantially self-contained while remaining part of a cooperative village.

## Group structure
A group may contain:
- hunters;
- gatherers;
- fishers;
- weavers;
- toolmakers;
- animal handlers;
- scouts/watchers;
- food processors.

The group can operate independently.

## Communal separatism
Groups maintain independence without isolation.

They:
- share surplus;
- lend labor;
- compensate for failed hunts or gathering;
- assist during major tasks;
- support one another after injury or loss.

Working principle:
> **Keep your group alive. Keep the village alive.**

## Household
Daily household life centers on the 12–15-person group rather than the entire village.

## Children
Children are raised primarily within their group while being known and supported by the larger village.

Children can learn from adults outside their immediate group through work exchanges and seasonal gatherings.

## Temporary work exchange
Groups can temporarily exchange workers and learners.

A skilled person may spend a season helping another group and then return.

This spreads knowledge without dissolving group identity.

## Partnership
Working proposal: partnerships occur primarily within the broader village and nearby network rather than being restricted to the small functional group.

The system should prevent the 12–15-person groups from becoming closed social/genetic islands.

Exact marriage mechanics remain open.

## Seasonal gatherings
Large wet-season gatherings provide opportunities for people from multiple villages to meet, maintain relationships, and form partnerships.

# 4. DESERT — WORKING PROFILE

## Social unit
Extended family households exist within oasis communities.

The oasis is the larger survival community, while the Lifekeeper network connects multiple oases.

## Partnership
Working proposal: partnerships commonly connect different oasis communities, reinforcing the larger network.

Unlike the Mountains, partnership is not restricted to the same specialist lineage.

## Residence
Couples may remain in one partner's oasis or move according to:
- family needs;
- community needs;
- water/resource conditions;
- travel obligations.

## Lifekeeper effect
Because Lifekeepers travel between oases, they naturally create opportunities for relationships and family connections across the desert.

## Children
Children belong to their family and oasis community.

Children showing unusual aptitude for travel, hunting, endurance, judgment, or environmental awareness may eventually enter Lifekeeper training.

## Water Watchers
Major oases may have trusted **Water Watchers**.

They do not own or control water.

They ensure the oasis remains usable by:
- checking water quality;
- monitoring levels;
- maintaining collection points;
- protecting drinking areas from contamination;
- maintaining emergency reserves;
- coordinating rationing during scarcity.

Their authority regulates **use**, not access.

During abundance, people take what they need.

During scarcity, Water Watchers establish practical limits so the community survives.

A stranded traveler is not denied life-saving water, but may receive enough to survive rather than enough to exhaust the oasis.

## Lifekeepers and Water Watchers
The two groups exchange information.

Lifekeepers bring information about distant:
- water conditions;
- failing oases;
- animal movements;
- weather;
- routes.

Water Watchers communicate:
- local water availability;
- rationing conditions;
- reserve status.

## Cultural principle
> **Water is shared, but survival requires discipline.**

# 5. COAST — WORKING PROFILE

## Social unit
The community itself is the practical household.

Coastal people live communally rather than in separate nuclear-family households.

## Housing
Large communal structures contain shared sleeping areas.

Most adults sleep together on mats in a large common space.

Children have a protected section within the communal structure.

There are no ordinary individual bedrooms.

A small number of enclosed private spaces exist for genuinely private activities, including reproduction and intimate partnership.

These are community spaces, not personal property.

## Parentage
Biological parentage is recognized.

However, parentage does not define exclusive responsibility.

Any adult can legitimately regard a community child as their child in the social sense.

## Child-rearing
Children are raised communally.

Adults collectively:
- supervise;
- teach;
- discipline;
- feed;
- protect;
- train;
- support.

Children can maintain strong individual bonds with parents and other adults without parenting becoming a private household responsibility.

## Partnership
Working proposal: partnerships exist within the communal structure and do not automatically create separate households.

The community remains the primary domestic unit.

## Privacy
Privacy exists as a specific social need rather than as everyday household separation.

People can seek enclosed spaces when needed, while ordinary life remains highly communal.

## Cultural principle
> **Almost everything is shared; privacy exists when it is needed.**

# 6. PLAINS — WORKING PROFILE

## Social unit
Flexible household plus mobile group.

Households contain family members, but daily survival often occurs through traveling groups.

## Partnership
Working proposal: partnership is influenced by community need, mobility, and family relationships.

The system should preserve Plains cooperation without turning marriage into rigid occupational assignment.

## Residence
Couples can remain with their existing group or join another group where their labor is needed.

## Children
Children are raised within the broader community.

They observe adult work from infancy and gradually acquire useful skills according to developmental stage and community need.

## Kinship
Family remains important, but mobility distributes kin across multiple communities.

## Hospitality
Guest-right is a strong cultural institution.

A peaceful traveler entering a Plains community is temporarily protected by that community.

The guest is expected not to endanger or exploit the people providing protection.

## Cultural principle
> **If you need shelter today, you may be the person providing it tomorrow.**

**Scope:** Plains, Wetlands, Desert, and Coast.  
**Status:** Working proposals derived from established Hearth cultural canon. Review before lock.

# 1. PLAINS

## Core social structure

Plains society operates across several overlapping layers:

1. household / family life;
2. mobile work and travel groups;
3. recurring anchor communities;
4. the broader Plains cultural network.

The mobile group is usually around 10–25 people, with 10–15 a common size. These groups are flexible rather than permanently familial. People can move between groups according to need.

Anchor communities are repeatedly used places where elders who can no longer travel, very young children, injured people, craft workers, teachers, and stored resources can remain.

This fits the established Plains pattern of being highly mobile without making every person permanently nomadic. fileciteturn92file3L1-L20

## Family and household

The family remains an important intimate unit, but it does not control a person's entire daily life.

A parent may sleep with family while spending most of the day with a hunting, gathering, scouting, crafting, or travel group.

Elders remain socially valuable even when they can no longer travel. Their role shifts toward:
- teaching;
- childcare;
- food processing;
- craft;
- storytelling;
- knowledge keeping;
- coordinating the anchor community.

## Partnership

**Working proposal:** Plains partnership is flexible and need-oriented rather than rigidly assigned to occupations.

A person can meet a partner through:
- their home community;
- a traveling group;
- another community encountered during seasonal movement;
- a recurring gathering;
- existing kin networks.

The partnership does not automatically determine permanent residence.

## Residence

New partners can remain with an existing community, join another traveling group, or move toward an anchor community when their skills or family responsibilities make that useful.

This prevents marriage from fighting the Plains mobility system.

## Children

Children are primarily attached to their family but are raised inside the larger community.

Very young children may remain near the anchor community while older children increasingly accompany mobile groups when capable.

Training follows developmental stage and community need rather than a fixed universal curriculum.

Children observe adult work constantly and gradually take on useful responsibilities.

## Kinship

Because people move between groups and communities, kinship can be geographically distributed.

A person may have close relatives in several communities without living with them.

This makes the broader Plains cultural network important: family ties, hospitality, shared stories, routes, and ecological knowledge cross community boundaries.

## Guest-right

A peaceful traveler entering a Plains community receives temporary protection and hospitality.

The obligation is reciprocal in spirit: anyone may someday be the traveler who needs shelter.

The guest is still expected not to endanger, exploit, or deliberately expose the host community to danger.

## Separation

Working proposal: partnerships can end without forcing the entire social network to break apart.

Because households are embedded in mobile groups and communities, family relationships can continue even when two partners no longer live together.

## Cultural principle

> **If you need shelter today, you may be the person providing it tomorrow.**

# 2. WETLANDS

## Core social structure

Wetland society is **communal separatist**.

The primary daily unit is a self-contained functional group of roughly 12–15 people.

A village may contain several such groups. Each can operate independently, but groups deliberately help one another.

## Family and household

The 12–15-person group is the practical household/survival unit.

It contains multiple functions rather than one hereditary specialization:
- hunting;
- gathering;
- fishing;
- weaving;
- toolmaking;
- animal handling;
- scouting/watch;
- food processing.

The group can survive independently, but it is not socially isolated.

## Abundance sharing

Surplus is expected to move between groups.

If Group A has a successful hunt while Group C has failed, A shares with C.

If Group B needs additional weaving labor and A has people available, A helps B.

This is not charity. The village is treated as a larger survival system.

Working principle:

> **Keep your group alive. Keep the village alive.**

## Leadership

**Working proposal:** leadership is temporary and task-based.

The person most trusted for the current problem coordinates the group:
- experienced hunter during a dangerous hunt;
- experienced builder during construction;
- best flood reader during seasonal relocation;
- skilled gatherer during a difficult harvest.

The same person does not automatically lead every activity.

This follows the Wetland emphasis on distributed competence rather than permanent hierarchy.

## Partnership

Partnership should prevent the 12–15-person groups from becoming closed genetic islands.

**Working proposal:** partners are generally drawn from the broader village or nearby groups rather than being restricted to one's own functional group.

The small group remains the daily unit, but marriage creates connections between groups.

## Residence

A couple can remain in one partner's group or move to another group.

Population, skill balance, existing kinship, and group needs can influence the decision.

Because groups already cooperate, moving between them is not culturally equivalent to abandoning one's people.

## Children

Children belong to their family and functional group but are known and supported by the whole village.

They learn by:
- observing adults;
- helping with small tasks;
- playing skill-building games;
- rotating through work;
- spending time with adults outside their immediate group.

The larger village therefore acts as a secondary teaching network.

## Seasonal exchange

Groups can temporarily exchange workers and learners.

A skilled weaver might spend a season helping another group.
A young person may spend part of a season learning from another group.
A hunter may assist a group that has lost one temporarily.

People return unless a longer-term need or partnership gives them reason to stay.

This spreads knowledge without dissolving group independence.

## Marriage and seasonal gatherings

Large wet-season gatherings create opportunities for partnerships across multiple villages.

These gatherings are especially useful because several villages may temporarily occupy the same high ground during monsoon conditions.

The gathering is therefore both an environmental adaptation and a social connector.

## Cultural principle

> **Your group can stand alone. Your village should never have to.**

# 3. DESERT

## Core social structure

Desert society consists of oasis communities linked by mobile travelers, especially the Lifekeepers.

The desert creates isolation through **distance and water scarcity**, while the Lifekeeper network actively reconnects the population. This distinction from Mountain isolation is established canon. fileciteturn92file5L1-L14

## Family and household

Working proposal: oasis residents generally live in extended family households smaller than the full oasis community.

The household handles intimate family life while the oasis handles shared survival infrastructure.

## Partnership

**Working proposal:** partnerships commonly connect different oasis communities.

This does not create a specialist caste.

Instead, it gives families relationships across the desert and strengthens the same network that Lifekeepers already maintain.

## Partner selection

The process is practical but not as formally controlled as the Mountain system.

Families may introduce potential partners through:
- known oasis relationships;
- Lifekeeper connections;
- major gatherings;
- visiting families;
- existing kin networks.

The prospective partners should have meaningful input once they are of appropriate age.

## Residence

A couple may remain in one partner's oasis or move to another.

Factors include:
- family needs;
- oasis population;
- water availability;
- skills;
- travel obligations;
- Lifekeeper training or service.

## Children

Children belong to their household and oasis community.

They learn:
- water discipline;
- route awareness;
- environmental signs;
- animal behavior;
- survival skills;
- community responsibilities.

A child showing unusual aptitude may eventually enter Lifekeeper training.

The Lifekeeper institution is explicitly open to recruits from across the Desert population rather than restricted to one family or oasis. fileciteturn92file5L3-L8

## Lifekeepers and family networks

Because Lifekeepers rotate between oases, they become natural carriers of:
- news;
- family information;
- warnings;
- resource knowledge;
- stories;
- introductions.

A Lifekeeper can therefore know relatives and family connections across a large portion of the desert.

## Water Watchers

**Working proposal / established direction:** Water Watchers maintain the usability of an oasis.

They do not own water and do not simply deny access.

They:
- monitor water levels;
- check water quality;
- maintain collection areas;
- prevent contamination;
- protect reserves;
- establish rationing when necessary.

Their authority regulates **use**, not basic access.

During abundance:
> Take what you need.

During scarcity:
> Take what the oasis can afford.

A stranded traveler can receive life-saving water without being allowed to exhaust the community's reserve.

## Children and water responsibility

Children gradually learn water discipline.

As they mature, they are taught that wasting water is not merely careless; it can endanger everyone dependent on the oasis.

This makes Water Watchers respected practical authorities without turning them into rulers.

## Lifekeeper / Water Watcher relationship

Lifekeepers report conditions from other oases.

Water Watchers provide current local water information.

Together they form a distributed survival-information network.

## Cultural principle

> **Of the people, for the people.**

And, for water specifically:

> **Water is shared, but survival requires discipline.**

# 4. COAST

## Core social structure

The Coastal people are the strongest communal society on Hearth.

The **community itself is the practical household**.

Their communal living is not merely communal labor. They live, sleep, eat, learn, raise children, and defend themselves together.

## Housing

Large communal structures contain shared sleeping spaces.

Most adults sleep together on mats in a large common room.

Children have a protected section within the communal structure.

There are not individual bedrooms or private family rooms.

A small number of enclosed spaces are provided for genuine privacy, especially intimate partnership and reproduction.

These spaces are communal facilities rather than personal property.

## Family and parentage

Biological parentage is recognized.

However, parentage does not create exclusive responsibility.

A child has parents, but every adult can legitimately regard that child as one of their children in the social sense.

The community is responsible for the child's survival.

## Partnership

**Working proposal:** partnership does not create an independent household.

A couple remains part of the communal living structure.

Their relationship is personally meaningful, but it does not split the community into nuclear-family units.

## Partner selection

Working proposal: because Coastal villages are highly interconnected and generalist, partner selection can be comparatively flexible.

The community recognizes biological kinship and lineage, but partnership does not need to reinforce occupational specialization.

Prospective partners can develop relationships through everyday communal life.

## Privacy

Privacy is a specific architectural provision rather than a normal household arrangement.

People do not have private bedrooms.

When privacy is genuinely needed, an enclosed community space is available.

Ordinary life remains highly communal.

## Children

Children are raised communally.

Adults collectively:
- feed;
- supervise;
- protect;
- discipline;
- teach;
- train;
- comfort.

Children can form particularly strong bonds with their biological parents without the community treating other adults as outsiders.

## Education

Because everyone participates in everything, children encounter many skills naturally.

They can learn:
- fishing;
- gathering;
- hunting;
- land reading;
- guarding;
- weaving;
- toolmaking;
- food processing;
- preservation.

This follows the established Coastal generalist model.

## Communal labor

The same communal principle extends beyond childcare.

The whole community can move through tasks in waves:
- fishing;
- communal meal;
- weaving;
- tanning;
- tool repair;
- construction;
- preservation;
- defense.

The Coastal people survive by functioning like a coordinated social body.

## Food preservation

Access to salt and the ability to remove salt from seawater gives Coastal people unusually strong preservation options.

Salted fish and other preserved foods reduce dependence on immediately obtaining another large kill.

This does not eliminate danger or mobility pressures, but it gives the Coast a greater ability to buffer bad days.

## Cultural principle

> **Almost everything is shared; privacy exists when it is needed.**

A broader shorthand:

> **The village is the family.**

Extended family + bilateral kinship + river-wide kin network.

Oasis household + cross-oasis family network + Lifekeeper connectivity.

Communal household + communal child-rearing + shared living.
