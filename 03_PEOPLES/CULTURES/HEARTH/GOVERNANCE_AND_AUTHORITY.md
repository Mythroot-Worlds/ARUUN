# MYTHROOT WORLDS — HEARTH PEOPLES
## Leadership, Authority & Conflict Resolution — Working Profiles v0.1

**Status:** WORKING PROPOSALS — review individually before lock.

**Design rule:** Authority is not assumed to mean monarchy, bureaucracy, or permanent political office. Each system is derived from the culture's existing survival structure.

---

# I. PLAINS

## Leadership structure

Plains communities use **distributed, role-based leadership**.

There is no single person who automatically commands everyone.

Instead, authority follows the problem:
- Hunt leader for a hunt;
- Scout leader for reconnaissance;
- Craft leader for major craft work;
- Migration leader when movement decisions are required;
- Water/resource leader when a specific resource crisis requires coordination.

A respected community leader may exist, but their authority comes from trust rather than permanent ownership of power.

## Decision-making

The people most directly responsible for a decision have the strongest voice.

A hunt leader makes immediate hunting decisions.

A scout can overrule ordinary assumptions about danger because their job is to identify danger.

A migration decision involves hunters, scouts, gatherers, elders, and others whose knowledge matters.

## Trust

Trust is the central currency of Plains authority.

A person who repeatedly makes good decisions gains influence.

A person who repeatedly endangers people loses it.

## Conflict

Disputes are initially handled by the relevant work group.

If a hunter is accused of making a dangerous decision, the community examines not only the individual but the hunting group that trusted and followed that person.

This reflects the established Plains principle that responsibility can belong to a group as well as an individual.

## Consequences

The response depends on the severity of the outcome.

Possible responses:
- restitution;
- temporary loss of decision-making authority;
- reassignment;
- removal from a dangerous role;
- group-level loss of trust;
- exclusion in extreme cases.

The goal is not revenge.

The question is:

> **Can this person still be trusted with other people's lives?**

## Cultural principle

**Don't get caught slipping. Trust has to be earned every day.**

# II. MOUNTAINS

## Leadership structure

Mountain authority is strongly tied to **specialist houses and lineage heads**.

Each specialist house has a recognized head who coordinates:
- training;
- specialist population;
- resource allocation;
- marriages;
- disputes within the lineage;
- representation at the Mountain Conclave.

The village may also have a coordinating elder or council of house heads.

## Authority

Authority is strongest within one's specialty.

A Hunter House head has substantial authority over hunters.

That does not mean the Hunter House head commands the Weaver House.

Major village decisions require the relevant houses to coordinate.

## Village leadership

The village functions through negotiation among specialist houses.

A decision affecting everyone may require agreement or broad support from multiple house heads.

The system is therefore hierarchical **inside lineages** but collaborative **between lineages**.

## Conclave authority

The annual Mountain Conclave is the highest regional coordinating institution.

It handles:
- marriage exchanges;
- specialist population balancing;
- inter-village disputes;
- trade;
- knowledge exchange;
- major regional problems.

It does not necessarily function as a permanent government.

## Conflict

A dispute involving a specialist begins within that specialist's house.

The relevant house head investigates.

If another house is involved, representatives of both houses meet.

Village-wide or inter-village disputes can escalate to the village council or Mountain Conclave.

## Consequences

Possible outcomes:
- restitution;
- temporary removal from responsibility;
- reassignment;
- loss of trust;
- removal from a specialist role;
- exile in extreme cases.

Because knowledge is tied to lineage, permanent punishment that destroys a useful specialist is undesirable unless necessary.

## Cultural principle

**The house preserves the knowledge. The village preserves the people.**

# III. RIVER

## Leadership structure

River communities use **situational leadership with strong elder and knowledge-holder influence**.

There is likely a recognized settlement coordinator, but the position is not absolute.

Different people become authoritative when their knowledge is relevant:
- river-reader during flood danger;
- experienced boat handler during crossings;
- hunter during dangerous hunts;
- elder during family disputes;
- experienced gatherer during resource decisions.

## River corridor leadership

No single river settlement controls the entire river.

Instead, communities maintain a network of recognized leaders who communicate information upstream and downstream.

A settlement coordinator may pass:
- flood warnings;
- resource information;
- disease warnings;
- animal movement;
- travel information;
- major disputes.

## Decision-making

Normal decisions are local.

River-wide problems require communication among multiple settlements.

The River people therefore develop an early form of **distributed coordination** without centralized government.

## Conflict

Local disputes begin within the household or settlement.

If a dispute involves people from different communities, representatives from both communities meet.

Because long-term river relationships matter, preserving future cooperation is often more important than humiliating an opponent.

## Restitution

River justice emphasizes restoring relationships.

Possible outcomes:
- returning stolen goods;
- replacing damaged property;
- repairing a boat;
- providing labor;
- compensating another family;
- temporarily separating disputing parties.

Serious violence can trigger broader community involvement.

## Reputation

A person's reputation can travel downstream and upstream.

Repeatedly dishonest behavior can therefore damage a person's ability to receive hospitality or trade.

## Cultural principle

**A broken relationship travels farther than a broken boat.**

# IV. WETLANDS

## Leadership structure

Wetland leadership is **temporary, functional, and group-centered**.

There is no permanent leader for every task.

The most knowledgeable person leads the relevant activity:
- hunter during a hunt;
- gatherer during difficult harvesting;
- builder during construction;
- flood-reader during seasonal relocation;
- experienced negotiator during inter-group disputes.

## Group decisions

Each 12–15-person group is substantially autonomous.

Internal decisions are made by the people who live and work together.

The group can reject outside direction unless the larger village is facing a genuine shared emergency.

## Village coordination

When several groups need to cooperate, representatives from the affected groups meet.

No single group automatically outranks the others.

A person can be highly influential in one situation and ordinary in another.

## Conflict

Disputes begin inside the group.

If unresolved, the issue moves to representatives from multiple groups.

Because the groups depend on one another for surplus and labor exchange, unnecessary escalation is culturally dangerous.

## Consequences

The primary goal is restoration.

Possible responses:
- repayment;
- replacement labor;
- temporary reassignment;
- temporary separation;
- loss of trust;
- removal from a particular responsibility.

Permanent exclusion is reserved for people who repeatedly endanger the group or deliberately undermine communal survival.

## Cultural principle

**Your group stands on its own, but your choices affect the village.**

# V. DESERT

## Leadership structure

Desert society has several forms of **specialized practical authority**, but no single authority controls everything.

### Oasis elders
Guide family and community decisions.

### Water Watchers
Have authority over safe and sustainable water use.

They regulate water use without owning or politically controlling the oasis.

### Lifekeepers
Have unusual authority regarding:
- travel;
- hunting;
- distant conditions;
- environmental danger;
- information from other oases.

Their authority is based on demonstrated competence.

## Water Watcher authority

A Water Watcher can say:

> “The oasis can support one barrel today.”

That instruction carries significant weight because ignoring it can endanger everyone.

But Water Watchers cannot simply declare:

> “You are not allowed to drink.”

Their authority is stewardship, not ownership.

## Lifekeeper authority

Lifekeepers can make immediate decisions during dangerous travel situations.

Within an oasis, however, they do not become rulers simply because they are respected hunters.

Their prestige is practical.

## Decision-making

Ordinary community matters remain with families and oasis elders.

Resource emergencies bring together:
- elders;
- Water Watchers;
- Lifekeepers;
- other relevant specialists.

## Conflict

Most disputes begin with families.

Community-level disputes are brought to elders.

Water disputes involve Water Watchers.

Travel or hunting disputes can involve Lifekeepers.

Major disputes can require a broader gathering.

## Desert justice

The most powerful punishment may be **loss of trust**.

A person who repeatedly wastes water, lies about environmental danger, or endangers travelers can become someone others refuse to rely upon.

Because survival depends on cooperation, reputation is extremely important.

## Cultural principle

**Those who know the danger have a responsibility to warn the people.**

# VI. COAST

## Leadership structure

Coastal people are the most strongly communal, so leadership should not resemble a chiefdom.

There may be respected coordinators, but **no individual owns the community's authority**.

Leadership emerges from demonstrated competence and communal trust.

## Task leadership

Because the whole community works in waves, each activity can have a coordinator:
- fishing coordinator;
- construction coordinator;
- food-preservation coordinator;
- defense coordinator;
- child-learning coordinator.

These people organize the work but do not become rulers.

## Elder authority

Elders have substantial influence because they possess accumulated knowledge.

Their authority is advisory and social rather than coercive.

An elder can say:

> “We've seen this before. Don't do that.”

People listen because the elder has survived long enough to know what they're talking about.

## Communal decisions

Major decisions are discussed openly among the community.

The goal is broad agreement rather than one person winning an argument.

Because everyone depends on everyone else, major decisions that leave half the community alienated are considered dangerous.

## Conflict

Minor disputes are handled socially and quickly.

People who know both sides can intervene before a disagreement grows.

Serious disputes are brought before a group of respected adults/elders.

The community considers:
- what happened;
- who was harmed;
- whether the act was deliberate;
- whether the person can be trusted;
- what must be done to repair the harm.

## Consequences

Possible consequences:
- apology;
- restitution;
- repair work;
- temporary removal from a responsibility;
- temporary separation;
- communal loss of trust.

Because the community is so interconnected, social consequences can be powerful without requiring harsh physical punishment.

## Cultural principle

**Nobody rules the community; the community carries itself.**

# VII. COMPARATIVE AUTHORITY MODEL

| People | Primary authority model |
|---|---|
| Plains | Trust + role-based leadership |
| Mountains | Specialist-house hierarchy + inter-house council |
| River | Situational leadership + distributed river network |
| Wetlands | Temporary functional leadership |
| Desert | Specialized practical authority + elders |
| Coast | Communal consensus + task coordinators |

# VIII. MAJOR OPEN QUESTIONS FOR REVIEW

These profiles intentionally leave some details open for later discussion:

1. Exact Plains leadership hierarchy, if any.
2. Whether Mountain villages have a permanent senior council.
3. How River settlements coordinate during continent-scale emergencies.
4. Whether Wetland groups ever grant someone long-term authority.
5. Whether Desert elders can override Water Watchers or Lifekeepers.
6. Whether Coastal consensus requires unanimity, majority agreement, or a strong consensus threshold.
7. Exact punishments for severe crimes.
8. Treatment of intentional murder.
9. Treatment of repeated negligence.
10. Inter-community warfare and diplomacy.

These should be resolved after reviewing the basic authority models rather than assumed now.

## Mountain Leadership & Specialist-House Update v0.1
**Status:** WORKING UPDATE — NOT CANON

Mountain authority remains tied to specialist houses and lineage heads.

Because settlements are now modeled at **50–70 people**, houses are understood to maintain **multiple related competencies** rather than one narrow occupation. House heads coordinate training, specialist knowledge, relevant resources, marriages, and representation at the Mountain Conclave.

The exact number and composition of houses remain **OPEN**.

The annual Mountain Conclave continues to coordinate marriage exchange, specialist population balancing, knowledge exchange, trade, and regional problems.

**No specific house count, specialty bundle, or 60-person target is canon.**
