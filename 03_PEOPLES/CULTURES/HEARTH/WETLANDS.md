# HEARTH — WETLAND PEOPLES
## Cultural Base v0.1

**Status:** WORKING CANON / PEOPLES PHASE

### Core philosophy
> **The environment changes. So we change with it.**

The Wetland peoples inherit cooperative elements of the Great Plains, but adapt them to changing water levels, channels, marshes, fish movements, seasonal plants, storms, and dangerous wetland terrain.

### Communities
Normal communities: **~80–120 people**. They favor elevated ground, natural islands, ridges, stable banks, and other reliable locations. A community thinks of its territory as a network of usable places rather than one permanent settlement.

### Seasonal high-ground gatherings
Three or four villages may occupy a broad territory, for example within ~25 km of one another. During major wet-season/monsoon flooding they can converge on one shared high-ground location, temporarily bringing together roughly **250–500 people**.

The gathering is environmentally driven and becomes a major exchange point for marriage, information, tools, techniques, stories, games, food processing, and inter-community relationships. When conditions improve, communities disperse. The high ground is culturally important through repeated use without automatically being sacred.

### Mobility and work
Mobility is local and seasonal rather than the long-distance movement typical of the Plains. People shift among known productive locations as water and resources change.

Functional groups can form around fishing, gathering, hunting, water scouting, marsh tracking, boat/raft handling, food processing, construction/repair, and watch. Membership changes with season and need.

### Children
Children learn through observation, imitation, play, useful participation, and progressive training. Water competence is taught early and safely: swimming/floating, currents, unstable ground, aquatic plants, animal signs, marsh movement, basic fishing, and water emergencies.

### Environmental knowledge
They closely monitor water level, current, channel direction, animal and bird behavior, insects, plants, weather, smell/water quality, and unusual absences. A place that looks unchanged can become dangerous quickly.

### Hospitality
They retain strong cooperative hospitality. Peaceful outsiders receive practical help and are taught how to stay alive in the wetlands: safe paths, dangerous channels, rising water, reeds, and animal hazards. Hospitality does not equal automatic trust.

### Patience
A defining virtue is **patience**: knowing when not to force the environment. People may wait for water to fall, fish to arrive, mud to firm, plants to mature, storms to pass, or a channel to become safe.

Working principle:
> **Adapt before you resist.**

### River relationship
Wetland and River cultures likely share an ancient aquatic vocabulary, stories, technologies, and traditions. Neither needs to know which came first. Boundary communities may be difficult to classify as purely River or Wetland.

Tentative contrast:
- River: **flow**
- Wetland: **change**

### Plains relationship
Shared: cooperation, distributed knowledge, hospitality, flexible work groups, need-driven training.
Difference: Wetland survival emphasizes adapting to a changing water landscape rather than following resources across enormous horizontal distances.

### Density distinction
Great Plains communities can be somewhat larger, potentially up to ~200 people, because broad meat, grain/seed, and plant abundance can support greater concentration. Wetland communities remain ~80–120 but periodically form much larger gatherings when environmental conditions force them onto shared high ground.
