# Mythroot Prehistoric World
## Human Evolution Research Track — v0.1

**Status: OPEN / FUTURE RESEARCH**

This document preserves the evolutionary research framework developed during early worldbuilding. It is not canon and establishes no physical traits for the world's native humanlike species.

## Purpose

Human evolutionary design will be researched only after the planetary and ecological environment has been sufficiently established.

The goal is not to create "superhuman prehistoric people." The goal is to determine what a humanlike species could plausibly become when shaped by this world's physical environment, ecology, culture, and evolutionary history.

## Core Principle

> **Evolution should emerge from the environment and from the species' interaction with that environment, rather than being designed trait-by-trait for coolness.**

Familiarity should come from convergent function rather than copied ancestry.

## Research Gate

Do not finalize human anatomy before establishing enough of:

1. Planetary parameters
2. Star and atmosphere
3. Geography
4. Climate
5. Biomes
6. Flora
7. Fauna
8. Predation/prey pressures
9. Food and subsistence
10. Disease/parasites
11. Mobility
12. Settlements
13. Technology/material culture
14. Social organization
15. Cultural adaptations
16. Environmental comforts and self-created niches

## Evolutionary Pressure Map

### 1. Physical Environment
Research pressures from gravity (~1.25g), atmosphere, stellar radiation, climate, temperature, humidity, wind, terrain, vegetation, and water.

Investigate possible effects on:
- bone density/architecture
- joints/connective tissue
- musculature
- stature/proportions
- posture/locomotion
- skin/hair
- respiratory anatomy
- vision
- thermoregulation

No trait is assumed merely because a pressure exists.

### 2. Predation & Environmental Danger
Investigate pressures from large predators, dangerous herbivores, falls, storms, floods, fire, earthquakes, volcanic activity, dense vegetation, and other hazards.

Potential areas:
- threat detection
- motion perception
- hearing/smell
- reaction speed
- endurance
- injury resistance
- clotting/infection resistance
- recovery

### 3. Hunting & Gathering
Investigate tracking, travel, climbing, swimming, carrying, tools, food processing, hunting, scavenging, plant identification, and seasonal resources.

Sensory traits should be treated as tradeoffs, not universal upgrades.

For example:

> **Broad-field perception and fine-detail perception may represent different evolutionary solutions rather than one species simply having "better eyesight."**

### 4. Energy & Efficiency
Investigate efficient walking/running, heat management, energy storage, digestion, sleep, repetitive movement, recovery, and economical metabolism.

Evolution should shape normal-life efficiency, not just emergency survival.

### 5. Social Evolution
Investigate communication, signaling, empathy, individual recognition, memory, cooperation, trust, conflict resolution, childcare, group cohesion, pair bonding, and social learning.

The species is a social animal evolving inside a community.

### 6. Reproduction & Development
Investigate gestation, childbirth, infant vulnerability, maturation, fertility, lifespan, parental investment, cooperative child-rearing, and sexual selection.

### 7. Culture & Gene-Culture Interaction
Humans modify their environment through fire, shelter, clothing, food processing, cooking, pottery, tools, medicine, ritual, settlements, storage, and early metallurgy.

Working model:

**Environment → behavior → culture → technology → modified environment → new evolutionary pressures**

### 8. Comfort & Self-Created Niches
Evolution is not only about avoiding death.

Investigate how shelter, warmth, prepared food, clothing, safer routes, social companionship, protected children, and predictable resources alter the selective environment.

### 9. Preference & Sexual Selection
Investigate mate choice, attraction, social status, display, cultural preference, and traits that become desirable after originating for another reason.

## Evolutionary Tradeoff Rule

The species should not receive every advantageous trait simultaneously.

Improvements can carry costs in energy, development, mobility, heat management, reproduction, nutrition, or other capabilities.

The objective is a plausible evolutionary compromise, not biological optimization.

## Research Classification

**Strongly Supported** — strong reason to expect the trait.

**Plausible** — compatible with known evolution but not inevitable.

**Speculative** — possible but requires additional assumptions.

**Rejected** — conflicts with biology, biomechanics, ecology, or established parameters.

## Final Design Test

Before locking a human trait, ask:

1. What pressure favors it?
2. What pressure opposes it?
3. What cost does it carry?
4. Does it improve survival or reproduction?
5. Could culture reduce or increase the pressure?
6. What secondary consequences follow?
7. Does it remain recognizably humanlike?
8. Does it fit the established ecology?
9. Does it feel evolved rather than designed?
10. Does it preserve the world's prehistoric identity?

## Current Working Direction

These are **research questions, not canon traits**:

- somewhat shorter stature
- greater skeletal robustness
- stronger connective tissues
- greater physical resilience
- altered skin structure
- altered hair distribution
- altered respiratory anatomy
- altered visual capabilities
- endurance adaptations
- improved injury/recovery physiology

None should be locked until the world parameters have been established and relevant science researched.

## Process Rule

The evolutionary research happens later.

Current sequence:

**Planet → Geography → Climate → Biomes → Flora → Fauna → Human Environment → Human Evolution → Culture/Civilization**

This is a scaffold, not a rigid workflow. New ideas may cause temporary movement backward or sideways.

> **The order organizes the work; it does not imprison the creative process.**
