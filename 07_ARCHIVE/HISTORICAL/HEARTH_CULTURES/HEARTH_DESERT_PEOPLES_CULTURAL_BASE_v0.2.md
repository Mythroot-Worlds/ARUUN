# HEARTH — DESERT PEOPLES
## Cultural Base v0.2 — Oasis Network Integrated

**Status:** WORKING CANON / PEOPLES PHASE

## Core philosophy

The Desert peoples resemble the Mountain peoples in some social pressures, but their isolation comes from **distance and water scarcity** rather than terrain.

Working principle:

> **Survival belongs to the people who know where to go, when to go, and what not to reveal.**

They are self-reliant, internally cohesive, cautious with outsiders, and exceptionally knowledgeable about water and routes.

## Environmental foundation

Sunscour is a major **closed/endorheic basin**. Water from surrounding mountains and uplands drains inward through seasonal/perennial channels and ultimately accumulates in an inland lake/salt-basin system before evaporation.

The basin contains scattered permanent water, seasonal water, springs, groundwater-fed oases, mineral/salt accumulation, and broad areas that remain sparsely occupied.

The Desert culture is therefore built around a **water network**, not around a generic sea of sand.

## Oasis network

The current working hydrologic model contains:

- **5 major perennial oasis systems**
- **6–10 secondary spring/groundwater systems**
- numerous seasonal water sites
- many emergency/route water locations known through local environmental knowledge

Major oasis zones occur primarily along:
- mountain-front alluvial fans;
- fault-controlled discharge zones;
- canyon mouths;
- basin-margin structural boundaries;
- shallow-groundwater zones.

The deepest basin center is more likely to contain saline lakes, playas, mudflats, and seasonal marshes than reliable freshwater.

## Community structure

Desert communities are expected to be relatively small compared with abundant Plains populations.

Major oases can support **~50–120 people** where water and food productivity justify it.

Other communities may be smaller or seasonal.

The 105,000-person dry-interior allocation is concentrated around the water network rather than spread evenly across Sunscour.

## Two complementary lifestyles

### Oasis communities
Relatively stationary or semi-stationary.

They provide:
- stable food;
- storage;
- crafts;
- animal care;
- children/elder support;
- refuge.

### Desert travelers
Highly mobile.

They provide:
- distant resource knowledge;
- hunting;
- route knowledge;
- news;
- inter-oasis connections;
- environmental warnings.

The Desert people are therefore neither purely nomadic nor purely oasis-bound.

They are a **network culture of stationary water communities and mobile specialists/travelers**.

## Isolation

Desert isolation is logistical.

A nearby community can be effectively distant when crossing the terrain requires scarce water and precise timing.

This encourages strong internal cooperation and deep local knowledge.

## Knowledge specialization

The desert rewards specialized knowledge of:
- water locations;
- seasonal springs;
- groundwater signs;
- storm patterns;
- safe routes;
- shelter;
- edible plants;
- animal movement;
- dangerous terrain.

Knowledge may become concentrated in families or trusted groups, echoing the Mountain lineage model, but exact hereditary specialization remains open.

## Hospitality

A peaceful traveler may receive water, food, shelter, and immediate survival assistance.

But assistance does not automatically include revealing:
- hidden water sources;
- routes;
- caches;
- strategically important information.

Working distinction:

> **You may be kept alive without being given access to everything we know.**

This is a cultural direction, not final law.

## Daily rhythm

Desert activity follows temperature and light.

### Before dawn / early morning
Major travel, hunting, gathering, scouting, water movement, and animal work begin.

### Midday
Outdoor activity decreases sharply. People shelter in shade, rock shelters, caves, or constructed shade spaces.

This becomes a major social period for:
- eating;
- craft work;
- repair;
- teaching;
- planning;
- information exchange;
- rest.

### Late afternoon / evening
Outdoor activity resumes.

Travelers return, hunters report, resources are processed, animals are tended, and groups prepare for night.

### Night
Cooler temperatures make travel and some hunting attractive.

The stars become a practical navigation reference.

## The desert has a memory

The people preserve detailed knowledge of:
- old camps;
- shade;
- emergency water;
- seasonal springs;
- plant locations;
- safe routes;
- dangerous terrain;
- animal watering sites.

Children learn places as well as techniques.

The desert is not owned by the people.

It is **remembered by them**.

## Relationship to Mountain peoples

Both cultures may value:
- internal cohesion;
- specialized knowledge;
- preparedness;
- competence;
- careful trust.

But their environmental pressures differ:

**Mountains:** isolation through terrain.

**Desert:** isolation through distance and water scarcity.
