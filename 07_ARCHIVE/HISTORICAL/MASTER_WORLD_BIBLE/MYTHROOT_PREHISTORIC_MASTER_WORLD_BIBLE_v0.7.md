## LOCKED / ESTABLISHED

- The world is **very low magic**.
- Magic is **real** and can have genuine effects.
- Magic does **not** operate as a game system.
- There are **no spells, levels, mana pools, numerical effects, quantified healing, or other game-mechanical magic structures** in the world.

- Magic can help, stabilize, encourage, or improve the chances of recovery, but it **cannot guarantee recovery**.
- Magic can influence an outcome; **it cannot dictate the outcome**.
- Magic does not radically transform the world into a high-magic setting.

- Human survival depends heavily on **knowledge, cooperation, adaptation, and accumulated experience**.
- Large-animal hunting is dangerous even for highly skilled people.

- Injury matters. People survive, adapt, and may carry permanent consequences.
- A person who can no longer perform a previous role is not automatically useless; communities adapt roles around changed abilities.
- Ecology is intended to be a major source of culture, conflict, and story.
- Animals should not exist merely as decorative monsters or spectacle.
- The world is intended to support stories rather than function as an exhaustive encyclopedia.

# 2. Humanity

## 2.1 Human Position

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED**

Humanity is not the apex species.

Humans survive through:

- knowledge;
- cooperation;
- adaptation;
- preparation;
- accumulated experience;
- understanding of the environment.

A single person is vulnerable.

A community is considerably more capable.

## 2.2 Settlement Philosophy

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED**

Human settlements are not assumed to be truly safe.

People deliberately identify locations that are **dramatically safer than the available alternatives**.

Settlement decisions are based on accumulated observation of the environment, including:

- animal movement;
- migration patterns;
- predator territories;
- terrain;
- visibility;
- water;
- seasonal changes;
- natural barriers;
- escape routes;
- resource access.

A settlement may exist because dangerous animals rarely enter the area, because major migrations pass elsewhere, because the terrain makes approach difficult, or because the location provides several advantages simultaneously.

> **Humans do not make the world safe. They learn where it is safer to live.**

## 2.3 Environmental Knowledge as Infrastructure

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED**

Knowledge of a place is itself a form of human infrastructure.

A community's accumulated understanding of its surroundings may determine:

- where it settles;
- when it moves;
- when it hunts;
- where it gathers resources;
- when it avoids particular areas;
- how it prepares for seasonal changes;
- how it responds to animal movement.

This knowledge may be transmitted through:

- observation;
- teaching;
- apprenticeship;
- oral tradition;
- ritual;
- stories;
- repeated practice.

Loss of environmental knowledge can therefore threaten a community even when its physical structures remain intact.

## 2.4 Settlement Defenses

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLE**

Human settlements do not need to be fortified against every major creature.

Defense may instead rely heavily on:

- geography;
- natural barriers;
- visibility;
- warning systems;
- controlled approaches;
- escape routes;
- knowledge of animal behavior;
- timing;
- settlement placement.

Structures may exist to shape movement, protect vulnerable people, contain resources, or create warning zones rather than to physically stop the largest animals.

## 2.5 Settlement Knowledge as Cultural Wealth

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLE**

A community's most valuable asset may not be its buildings or material possessions.

It may be:

> **The knowledge that lets its people understand and survive the place they inhabit.**

Experienced people may therefore function as repositories of environmental knowledge.

Their value is not simply social or symbolic; their knowledge can have direct survival consequences.


## 2.6 Human Progress & Sustainable Scale

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLE**

> **Humanity's progress is constrained less by what it can understand than by what it can safely sustain.**

Human intelligence does not automatically produce large-scale technological development.

Technological and civilizational advances require the ability to sustain:

- labor;
- infrastructure;
- resource extraction;
- specialization;
- surplus;
- concentration of people;
- long-term safety.

The world's ecology can therefore limit the practical scale at which human knowledge can be applied.

Humans may understand a process without possessing the environmental stability required to exploit it extensively.

## 2.2 Civilization Scale

**PROVISIONAL / SEED**

The initial concept describes human civilization as small and scattered.

The exact population, settlement pattern, geographic distribution, and degree of civilization remain undefined.

## 2.3 Human Intelligence

**LOCKED / ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLE**

Limited technology does not imply limited intelligence.

Human societies may possess sophisticated:

- ecological knowledge;
- material knowledge;
- social knowledge;
- medicine;

# CURRENT CONSOLIDATION — 2026-08-22

## Planetary / Astronomical Direction
- Two moons are the selected configuration.
- The Great Moon is physically larger but farther away.
- The Near/Medium Moon is physically smaller but closer.
- Their apparent sizes should be relatively similar from the surface.
- Their differing distances and masses are intended to create distinct tidal cycles.
- The moons should interact meaningfully with the planet's tectonic system, but **must not be treated as the primary cause of continental drift**. Planetary interior/plate tectonic processes remain the fundamental driver.
- Lunar tidal stresses may interact with already-stressed faults, volcanic systems, plate boundaries, and other geological thresholds, potentially influencing when some geological events occur.
- Night is not a simple fixed darkness period. Illumination varies with solar geometry, atmospheric effects, terrain, moon position, and moon phase. A night may be bright, dim, or genuinely dark.
- A 32-Earth-hour rotation period is the current selected candidate; it is not yet separately locked unless explicitly confirmed.
- The warm/amber K-type star remains the leading candidate, not yet locked.
- ~26° axial tilt and moderate stable orbital eccentricity remain candidates, not locked.

## Ecological / Visual Direction
- The world should feel prehistoric before it feels alien.
- Vegetation generally favors robustness, breadth, density, and low-to-moderate growth rather than extreme vertical height.
- Trees are envisioned as relatively short, broad, thick, and rounded rather than towering redwood-like forms.
- Large swaths of thick grasses, shrubs, and ground vegetation are desired.
- "Lush" means biologically robust and dense, not necessarily tall.
- Vegetation colors vary by region and ecology. Purple is an example of a possible regional expression, not a planetary default.

## Scientific Design Rule
- Major differences from Earth should be grounded in causal chains: planetary conditions → environment → ecology → evolution → culture.
- Familiarity should come from convergent ecological function rather than copied Earth species.
- The world is not intended to become science fiction simply because its planet differs from Earth.

## Process / Canon Rule
- Only explicitly locked decisions become canon.
- Provisional concepts, research hypotheses, and brainstorming remain clearly separate from canon.
- The world is developed from broad questions toward increasingly specific detail, with periodic canon checkpoints.
- The workflow is a scaffold, not a rigid sequence; new ideas can cause temporary movement backward or sideways.

# XV. MOON MODEL VALIDATION — RESEARCH PASS

**Status:** Research-supported working model; exact parameters remain subject to final lock.

The selected balanced moon configuration was checked against the current planetary assumptions.

## Working planetary mass assumption

With a radius of roughly 1.5 Earth radii and surface gravity of roughly 1.25g, an Earth-like average density implies a planetary mass of roughly 2.8 Earth masses. This is an inference from current parameters, not an independently locked density.

## Great Moon

- Radius: ~1.4× Earth's Moon
- Distance: ~540,000 km
- Mass: ~2.74× Earth's Moon, assuming comparable density
- Orbital period: ~27.15 Earth days
- Apparent angular diameter: ~0.516°
- Tide-generating strength relative to Earth's Moon at Earth's distance: ~0.99×

## Near Moon

- Radius: ~0.7× Earth's Moon
- Distance: ~270,000 km
- Mass: ~0.343× Earth's Moon, assuming comparable density
- Orbital period: ~9.65 Earth days
- Apparent angular diameter: ~0.516°
- Tide-generating strength relative to Earth's Moon at Earth's distance: ~0.99×

## Key result

Because the Near Moon is approximately half the diameter of the Great Moon but half as far away, and comparable density is assumed, the selected proportions produce nearly equal tide-generating strength from the two moons.

This cleanly supports the desired setting concept:

> **The larger moon is farther away; the smaller moon is closer; both look about the same size and can have comparable tidal influence without either being absurdly exotic.**

## Orbital stability

A rough Hill-sphere check using a K-type star and plausible habitable-zone orbital distances places both proposed lunar orbits far inside the planet's broad prograde satellite-stability region. Recent multi-moon/exomoon stability work finds prograde satellite stability commonly extending to roughly 0.5 Hill radii, with exact limits depending on the system.

Under plausible stellar/orbital assumptions, the planet's Hill radius is on the order of millions of kilometers, while these moons orbit only hundreds of thousands of kilometers away.

**Conclusion:** no obvious Hill-sphere stability problem is present.

## Roche-limit check

Both proposed lunar orbits are vastly farther out than the expected Roche-limit region for rocky satellites.

**Conclusion:** neither moon is remotely close to immediate tidal disruption.

## Tidal evolution

Tidal dissipation transfers angular momentum between planetary rotation and satellite orbits. NASA's Earth-Moon research shows that tides can slow planetary rotation while transferring angular momentum into lunar orbital motion, and that the rate depends strongly on ocean depth, resonance, dissipation, continents, and other properties.

Therefore:
- the moons should not be assumed to have remained at their present distances forever;
- their long-term histories can differ from Earth's;
- changing continents and ocean basins can change tidal dissipation;
- the two-moon system can participate in the planet's long geological history.

## Tectonic interaction — scientific boundary

The moons do **not** directly drive continental drift.

The primary chain remains:

**planetary interior → plate tectonics → continental movement**

The lunar/solar contribution is:

**two moons + star → tidal forcing**

which can become:

**tidal forcing + an already stressed geological system → possible modulation of some fault/volcanic behavior**

Earth itself experiences solid-Earth tides, and tidal stresses can influence some seismic systems, although such effects are secondary and highly dependent on local conditions.

This preserves the intended worldbuilding idea that the moons **interact with** tectonic processes without replacing the physical mechanism of plate tectonics.

## Night illumination

The two moons also mean that "night" cannot be treated as a fixed 16-hour darkness period.

Actual darkness depends on:
- latitude;
- season;
- axial tilt;
- atmospheric scattering;
- cloud cover;
- terrain;
- moon altitude;
- moon phase;
- whether either moon is above the horizon.

The world therefore has:
**daylight → twilight → moonlit night → dark night**

rather than a simplistic day/night binary.

## Research conclusion

The balanced configuration is physically coherent enough to retain as the leading model.

It does not require:
- impossible moon sizes;
- impossible distances;
- immediate Roche-limit danger;
- an obviously unstable satellite arrangement;
- or a magical explanation for tides.

Still open before final lock:
- moon density/composition;
- orbital inclinations;
- orbital eccentricities;
- formation histories;
- exact long-term tidal evolution;
- exact stellar orbit and planetary Hill sphere;
- detailed tidal modeling once geography/ocean basins exist.

# XVI. STELLAR ORBIT / YEAR — RESEARCH-SUPPORTED WORKING MODEL

**Status:** Recommended working model; pending explicit canon lock.

## Star

The world uses a **warm K-type main-sequence star** as its leading stellar model.

This remains consistent with the project's desire for:
- an orange/amber-leaning sun;
- a long-lived stellar environment;
- a habitable world that can remain biologically active for a very long period;
- a surface light spectrum that differs from Earth's without requiring science-fiction physics.

NASA describes K dwarfs as cooler and less massive than G-type stars, with long main-sequence lifetimes and generally more favorable radiation environments for life than M dwarfs. NASA-backed climate modeling also finds K-dwarf systems capable of maintaining stable habitable climate states.

## Recommended Stellar Parameters

Use a warm K dwarf approximately in the neighborhood of:

- **Stellar mass:** ~0.80 solar masses
- **Luminosity:** ~0.40 solar luminosities
- **Spectral character:** warm K-type / orange-amber

These are worldbuilding targets, not a claim that one exact real star has these exact properties.

## Planetary Orbit

To keep surface energy broadly Earth-comparable while making the system measurably different, place the planet at approximately:

- **Semi-major axis:** ~0.65 AU
- **Orbital eccentricity:** ~0.08
- **Orbital period / year:** ~214 Earth days

Using Newtonian/Keplerian orbital relationships, a ~0.65 AU orbit around a ~0.80-solar-mass star produces a year of roughly 214 Earth days.

NASA's Kepler-law material confirms that orbital period depends on orbital distance and the masses involved.

## Why this works

At ~0.65 AU around a ~0.40-solar-luminosity star, the planet receives roughly Earth-comparable average stellar energy.

This is intentional.

We do **not** need to make the planet colder simply because its sun is cooler, nor do we need to put it at an Earth-like 1 AU distance.

The world can therefore have:
- a visibly different sun;
- a different spectrum;
- a shorter year;
- different seasonal timing;
- and broadly habitable surface energy.

## Year and Seasons

The working year is approximately **214 Earth days**.

With the existing candidate axial tilt of ~26°, seasonal changes can remain meaningful.

The moderate orbital eccentricity (~0.08) makes the planet's distance from its star change noticeably over the year without producing an extreme eccentric orbit.

This gives the climate system another source of seasonal asymmetry:

**axial tilt → latitude/seasonal effects**

plus

**orbital eccentricity → varying stellar distance and orbital speed**

The two effects do not need to reinforce one another everywhere.

## Important Scientific Boundary

The exact surface climate cannot be inferred from stellar luminosity alone.

Atmospheric composition, clouds, oceans, albedo, greenhouse gases, circulation, geography, and hydrological cycling all matter.

NASA climate modeling of Earth-like planets around K dwarfs explicitly treats the hydrological cycle and atmospheric/climate feedbacks as important to habitability.

Therefore the stellar orbit establishes the **energy framework**, not the final climate map.

## Prehistoric-World Fit

This model intentionally avoids making the planet exotic for the sake of exoticism.

The differences are subtle but consequential:

- orange/amber star instead of yellow Sun;
- ~214-day year instead of 365 days;
- 32-hour rotation instead of 24;
- ~26° axial tilt;
- moderate orbital eccentricity;
- two substantial moons.

None of these individually makes the world feel alien.

Together they create a world whose seasons, skies, nights, calendars, migration patterns, and ecological rhythms are recognizably natural but distinctly not Earth.

**Planetary scale:** ~1.5× Earth  
**Land/water target:** ~70/30  
**Rotation:** ~32 hours  
**Star:** warm K-type  
**Star mass:** ~0.80 solar masses  
**Star luminosity:** ~0.40 solar luminosities  
**Planet orbit:** ~0.65 AU  
**Year:** ~214 Earth days  
**Orbital eccentricity:** ~0.08  
**Axial tilt:** ~26° candidate  
**Moons:** one large/far + one smaller/near, approximately equal apparent size

**Status:** planetary system is now sufficiently specified to begin a first-pass geography/continental skeleton, while atmosphere and climate remain open research layers.

## Research basis

NASA sources consulted for this pass support:
- K-dwarf characteristics and long stellar lifetimes;
- K-dwarf habitability and climate behavior;
- the importance of stellar flux and hydrological feedback;
- Kepler/Newton orbital relationships between stellar mass, orbital distance, and orbital period.

This document records the worldbuilding synthesis rather than claiming that these exact values are observed in a real system.
