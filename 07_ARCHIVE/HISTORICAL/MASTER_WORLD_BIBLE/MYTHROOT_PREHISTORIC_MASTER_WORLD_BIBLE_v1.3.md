# 1. WORLD DESIGN PROFILE

## Core identity

A naturalistic, prehistoric-esque world that is fundamentally its own world rather than Earth transplanted into another timeline.

It is Earth-adjacent in recognizable biology and physical logic, but evolution, geography, climate, and history followed a different path.

The desired experience is:
- an ancient, old-world environment;
- primitive/early civilization;
- humanity living inside a powerful ecosystem;
- recognizable natural processes producing unfamiliar results;
- low-grade magic that does not turn the setting into conventional high fantasy.

## Design philosophy

For this world, science is a **design constraint and source of causality**, not an absolute creative master.

Where science can explain something naturally, prefer that explanation.

Where multiple scientifically plausible outcomes exist, choose the one that best serves the world.

Where a deliberate creative choice is more important, the choice may override strict realism.

The important distinction is:
- **Established premise** — what we decide the world has.
- **Derived consequence** — what follows from those premises.
- **Plausible option** — one scientifically defensible possibility.
- **Creative decision** — deliberately chosen for the setting.
- **Open/unknown** — not yet decided.

# 2. MAGIC & SPIRITUAL ECOLOGY

## Locked / established

- Magic is real but very low-grade.
- It is not a game system.
- There are no mana pools, levels, spell lists, numerical effects, or quantified healing.
- Rituals, chants, gestures, preparations, herbs, pastes, smoke, blood, and similar practices can have genuine effects.
- Magic can **restore**, but does not simply erase physical damage.
- Magic can help, stabilize, encourage, or improve the chance of recovery.
- Magic cannot guarantee an outcome.
- Magic can influence an outcome; it cannot dictate it.
- Material practices may have ordinary biological/environmental explanations, while a small spiritual presence also interacts with the natural world.
- The exact metaphysical mechanism remains open.

The setting's low-grade magic should remain compatible with a world whose dominant explanation for unusual phenomena is still natural/material.

# 3. PLANETARY FOUNDATION

Current working planetary parameters:

- ~1.25g gravity.
- Atmosphere broadly habitable to the world's human lineage.
- 32-hour day.
- ~26° axial tilt.
- ~214 Earth-day orbital period.
- ~0.08 orbital eccentricity.
- Warm K-type star.
- ~0.65 AU orbital distance.
- Roughly 70/30 land-water target.
- Two moons: one larger/farther, one smaller/closer, visually similar in apparent size.
- Two moons contribute secondary tidal/geological effects but do **not** directly push continents; internal planetary heat and mantle dynamics remain the fundamental tectonic driver.
- Sky may have a subtle greenish cast; exact atmospheric chemistry remains open.
- Vegetation and flames may differ in appearance from Earth where atmospheric chemistry and biology plausibly allow it.

# 4. HUMANITY

## Locked / established

- Humans are native to this world.
- They are human-like in broad body plan and social behavior but followed a distinct evolutionary path.
- They may be somewhat shorter/stockier than modern humans.
- Slightly greater bone density and physical resilience are plausible consequences of ~1.25g gravity.
- Human evolution should produce interesting but restrained differences, not arbitrary alien anatomy.
- Humanity is not the apex predator.
- Survival depends heavily on knowledge, cooperation, adaptation, and accumulated experience.
- Civilization is constrained less by what humans can understand than by what they can safely sustain.
- Human settlements are preferentially established where environments are **dramatically safer than surrounding alternatives**, not because any location is truly safe.
- Humans learn migration patterns, terrain, animal behavior, weather, resources, and other environmental information because knowledge equals survival.
- Large-animal hunting is dangerous even for skilled people.
- A successful hunt can still cause injury, death, lost labor, medical burden, and social consequences.

# 5. TECTONIC WORLD MODEL

**Status:** Research-informed working model. The architecture is established as the current worldbuilding foundation; exact vectors and cartographic boundaries remain refinable.

## Four major continental systems

1. **Hearth** — mostly intact great continent.
2. **Shattered** — enormous continental fragments separated by major seas.
3. **Rift** — one great continental system actively splitting into two major halves.
4. **Lost** — enormous continuous continent isolated by an immense ocean.

The three main continental systems are relatively close together. Lost is extremely isolated.

## Working seven-plate architecture

### Hearth Plate
Carries the mostly intact continent. Old, thick continental interior with active margins and small peripheral island systems.

### Shatter-West Plate
Carries major western/central fragments of Shattered. Long-lived rifting followed by transform/oblique movement produces large fragments and rotated geology.

### Shatter-East Plate
Carries eastern fragments. Its long transform/oblique boundary allows translation and rotation relative to Shatter-West.

### Rift Crown Plate
Carries one major half of Rift.

### Rift South Plate
Carries the other major half. The two Rift plates are diverging, but the rift has not matured into a full ocean.

### Oceanic Ring Plate
Major oceanic plate containing spreading ridges, subduction margins, and transform systems that organize the main continental cluster.

### Lost Plate
Carries Lost. It has remained geographically isolated for roughly 700 million–1 billion years as a working geological history while continuing its own tectonic evolution.

## Geological history

**Ancient continental assembly**
→ volcanic arcs, accretion, collisions, stabilization

**Supercontinental interval**
→ broad ancestral continental complex

**Lost separation**
→ major rift succeeds, new oceanic crust forms, Lost begins long isolation

**Main-cluster breakup**
→ Hearth, Shattered, and Rift systems emerge

**Shattering**
→ overlapping rifts + transform/oblique motion + rotation + accretion create enormous fragments

**Rift formation**
→ continental crust thins, faults and basins form, volcanism increases, but full ocean formation has not yet occurred

**Long reorganization**
→ spreading, subduction, transform motion, accretion, uplift, erosion

**Present**
→ four continental systems remain active geological participants.

# 6. GLOBAL CLIMATE MODEL

Climate is derived from planetary parameters plus geography rather than selected arbitrarily.

Causal chain:

**planetary parameters**
→ atmospheric circulation
→ ocean circulation
→ tectonic geography
→ elevation/mountains
→ prevailing winds
→ precipitation
→ temperature
→ climate
→ water systems
→ ecosystems

Broad expectations:
- meaningful seasons from ~26° axial tilt;
- shorter ecological year because of ~214-day orbit;
- ocean-driven heat redistribution;
- strong rain-shadow effects;
- stronger continentality in large interiors;
- a lush world without every region being wet;
- dense low vegetation, robust grasslands, wetlands, seasonal woodland, scrub, dry basins, and localized deserts.

# 7. HEARTH CONTINENT

## Geographic identity

One enormous, mostly continuous continental landmass.

Major features:
- old continental interior;
- broad plains and plateaus;
- major mountain systems toward active margins;
- large river basins;
- floodplains and wetlands;
- coastal plains and estuaries;
- small peripheral islands;

## Climate

### Coastal
Ocean-moderated. Depending on current and wind direction:
- humid forests;
- dense low vegetation;
- coastal grasslands;
- wetlands;

Wet windward slopes, forests, wet uplands, headwaters.

### Leeward

- grassland;

**Connected abundance.**

## Mapmaker brief

# 8. SHATTERED CONTINENT

## Geographic identity

A former continental system divided into several enormous continental fragments.

These are continental-scale islands, not a field of small islands.

Major features:
- one especially large western/central fragment;
- several enormous eastern/northern fragments;
- smaller peripheral islands;
- independent mountain systems;
- deep marine basins;
- major straits;
- isolated interior basins.

## Climate

Ocean influence is stronger than on Hearth.

### Largest fragment
Wet coasts, seasonal woodland, grasslands, dry forests, interior basins, localized semi-arid regions.

### Wetter fragments
Dense low forests, wetlands, productive rivers, foggy/stormy coasts.

### Drier eastern fragments
Dry woodland, grassland, scrub, seasonal rivers, dry basins, localized arid environments.

## Ecological identity
**Fragmentation and specialization.**

Shared ancestral life can develop regional variants because populations are separated by major water barriers.

## Established ecological seed
A future amphibious/marine-capable migratory species can migrate between major fragments.

Working behavior:
- wet season creates abundant food on a relatively dry eastern fragment;
- hot/dry season causes food and water decline;
- animals migrate westward toward a wetter fragment;
- migration may involve ~40 miles of open water;
- migration carries genuine mortality risk.

Appearance, name, anatomy, and other species details remain open.

## Mapmaker brief
Show several enormous continental fragments, one especially large western/central fragment, deep seas and straits, independent mountains, wet coastal zones, drier interiors, smaller islands, and geological relationships suggesting former connection without making the fragments clean puzzle pieces.

# 9. RIFT CONTINENT

Major features:

- long rift system;

- steep escarpments;
- rift lakes;
- inland seas;
- wetlands;
- volcanic fields/cones;
- highlands;

## Climate

### Highlands
Moisture capture produces:
- wet forests;
- cool uplands;
- dense vegetation;
- river headwaters.

### Rift floor
Lower and often warmer, with sections that are substantially drier:
- dry grasslands;
- seasonal woodland;
- warm lowlands;

### Lakes/wetlands
- permanent freshwater systems;

## Ecological identity
**Transition and instability.**

## Mapmaker brief
Show two enormous halves, irregular central rift, large lakes/inland seas, wetlands, steep escarpments, highlands, volcanic zones, dry rift-floor basins, wet uplands, and sections where the rift is narrow versus wide.

# 9. OCEAN CIRCULATION & MARINE CLIMATE — DETAILED WORKING MODEL

The ocean circulation model is now sufficiently defined to drive the climate map and ecological placement.

Scientific basis: surface currents are primarily wind-driven and modified by Coriolis and continental geometry; subtropical gyres rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere; western boundary currents are typically narrow/strong while eastern boundary currents are broader/weaker; equatorial circulation differs because Coriolis approaches zero at the equator. citeturn0search4turn0search5

## Global wind scaffold

- 0–5° from the equator: ITCZ/doldrum region; rising warm, moist air and frequent convection.
- ~5–30°: trade-wind belt, generally east-to-west at the surface.
- ~30–60°: prevailing westerlies, generally west-to-east.
- Coriolis deflects moving air and surface water right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere. citeturn0search0turn0search12

## Northern Subtropical Gyre

**Working name: Hearthward Gyre**

A clockwise subtropical circulation occupies the main northern ocean basin.

### Hearthward Western Current
- Warm, narrow, comparatively fast poleward current.
- Runs along the western side of the northern ocean basin.
- Raises coastal sea temperatures and increases moisture availability.
- Moderates nearby seasonal extremes.

### Hearthward Return Current
- Cooler, broader equatorward current on the eastern side of the basin.
- Creates a cooler maritime margin.
- Candidate for persistent coastal fog and seasonal upwelling where winds run parallel to shore.

### Climate consequence
Hearth and the northern Shattered fragments should not have symmetrical climates on their eastern and western coasts even at similar latitudes.

## Equatorial Current Belt

**Working name: Equatorial Drift**

Trade winds generally drive warm surface water westward across the equatorial ocean. This produces a warm western tropical pool and a cooler, more productive eastern tropical margin when upwelling is active. Earth's Pacific provides a useful real-world analogue. citeturn0search1

### Rift consequence
Because Rift crosses the equator, its marine climate is strongly affected by this belt.

Expect:
- warm western-facing tropical seas;
- seasonally shifting productive zones;
- localized upwelling;
- strong rainfall gradients;
- large lake/inland-sea ecosystems connected to the broader tropical circulation.

## Southern Subtropical Gyre

**Working name: Lostward Gyre**

A counterclockwise subtropical circulation occupies the major southern ocean basin.

### Lostward Western Current
- Warm, narrow, poleward current.
- Carries tropical/subtropical heat toward Lost's warmer-side coast.
- Supports warm maritime climates and high coastal productivity where shelves/estuaries are favorable.

### Lostward Return Current
- Cooler, broader equatorward current.
- Creates a cooler/drier maritime margin.
- Candidate for strong coastal upwelling.

## Major Upwelling Zones

Upwelling occurs when winds and Ekman transport move surface water away from a coast or region and deeper water rises to replace it. That deeper water is typically cold and nutrient-rich, making upwelling zones highly productive. citeturn0search2turn0search6

### Shattered — Eastern Cool-Coast Upwelling

A major Shattered fragment has a cool-current-facing coast aligned with prevailing winds.

Expected chain:

**wind → offshore surface transport → cold nutrient-rich water rises → plankton bloom → fish biomass → marine predators → coastal scavengers**

Consequences:
- one of the world's richest marine food zones;
- seasonal or persistent fog;
- cool coastal water despite relatively warm latitude;
- major fish/large-marine-animal concentrations;
- strong coastal-resource pulse.

This is also a major reason Shattered's western/wetter fragments can remain ecologically rich while other fragments experience severe seasonal drying.

### Lost — Cool-Coast Upwelling

Lost receives a second major upwelling system along a cool-current-facing coast.

Expected chain:

**deep nutrient input → plankton → fish → large marine predators → seabirds/coastal fauna → terrestrial nutrient transfer**

Because Lost is enormous, this zone can support a marine ecosystem of continental significance.

### Rift — Localized Productive Zones

Rift does not require a continent-scale permanent upwelling belt.

Its:
- irregular coastlines;
- enclosed seas;
- rift lakes;
- seasonal winds;
- volcanic margins;
- narrow basins

create multiple localized productivity hotspots.

## Downwelling

Where winds push surface water toward a coast, water can accumulate and sink.

Downwelling suppresses immediate surface productivity relative to upwelling, but it also transports oxygenated surface water downward and participates in the larger circulation system.

## Marine Productivity Belts

### Very high
- major upwelling coasts;
- river deltas/estuaries;
- shallow continental shelves;
- productive inland seas;
- current fronts where nutrient-rich and warm waters interact.

### Moderate/high
- temperate shelves;
- seasonal coastal mixing zones;
- reef/coastal ecosystems.

### Lower
- strongly stratified subtropical open-ocean interiors.

Nutrient delivery from land and deep water is a major driver of marine primary production and therefore higher marine food webs. citeturn0search34

## Deep Ocean

A slow density-driven circulation exists beneath the surface system.

Temperature and salinity differences affect seawater density and can drive deep circulation. citeturn0search11turn0search16

For Mythroot, the deep system is important as a background heat/nutrient transport mechanism, but its exact geometry is deliberately not numerically modeled yet.

## Continental Coastal Effects

### Hearth
- One coast: warmer/more maritime due to warm poleward current.
- Opposing coast: cooler, potentially foggier, with stronger upwelling potential.
- Interior: increasingly continental and seasonally extreme.
- Large rivers and shelves create additional productive marine zones.

### Shattered
- Strongest overall ocean influence.
- Fragment-to-fragment climate can differ dramatically.
- Warm/cool current contrasts are major drivers.
- Seasonal marine productivity can determine animal migrations.
- The Farwader's seasonal migration is therefore ecologically reinforced by ocean circulation.

### Rift
- Equatorial circulation dominates lowlands.
- Mountain barriers and rift geometry create sharp rainfall differences.
- Local marine/lake productivity hotspots are common.
- Volcanic and geothermal regions remain important local climate modifiers.

### Lost
- Enormous coastline means multiple marine climates.
- Warm-current coast supports humid/warm maritime ecosystems.
- Cool-current coast supports fog, cooler water, and major upwelling.
- Interior becomes increasingly continental with distance from ocean.
- Coastal productivity can support large concentrations of marine and terrestrial life.

## Human-Relevant Consequences

These currents create predictable later human geography:
- major fisheries near upwelling;
- settlements near estuaries and river mouths;
- seasonal coastal migrations;
- navigation corridors;
- dangerous current crossings;
- fog hazards;
- storm-exposed coasts;
- resource booms and crashes.

This layer is now sufficient to begin developing human settlement without inventing coastal climates independently.

# 24. HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY LAYER

The HPGL establishes where humans plausibly concentrate, disperse, migrate, and become isolated before named peoples are created.

## Working global population

**~3.5–4 million humans globally (provisional).**

- Hearth: ~1.5 million
- Shattered: ~800,000
- Rift: ~1.0 million
- Lost: ~300,000

These are working demographic values, not final locked numbers.

## Residential scale

Typical residential communities are provisionally modeled at **~80–150 people**, with smaller groups (~40–80) and larger seasonal aggregations (~200–500+) possible. A residential community is not necessarily a complete cultural population; multiple communities can share language, marriage networks, ecological knowledge, trade, and seasonal gathering sites.

## Mobility

Humans may be sedentary, semi-mobile, seasonally mobile, nomadic, coastal, river/lake oriented, herd-following, plant-season following, or mixed depending on local ecology. Large-animal migrations and seasonal plant, fish, fruit, seed, and wetland abundance can drive human migration.

## Population distribution

Humans are **not evenly distributed**. Population concentrates where food productivity, reliable water, tolerable climate, manageable terrain, and predictable seasonal resources overlap. Dangerous megafauna, extreme climate, difficult travel, and resource scarcity create large low-density or unoccupied regions.

## Continental patterns

**Hearth:** highest connectivity; population concentrates along rivers, grassland/woodland boundaries, wetlands, temperate coasts, and highland valleys. Gene flow is generally moderate-to-high along major corridors.

**Shattered:** fragmented populations with strong coastal and seasonal dependence. Wet western/ocean-influenced regions can act as dry-season refuges. Inter-island connectivity varies sharply according to crossing difficulty and marine conditions.

**Rift:** population follows rift-floor grasslands, lake margins, river corridors, wetlands, and localized highland valleys. Mountains, dry basins, and volcanic terrain create strong local isolation.

**Lost:** lowest population density and greatest potential for long-term isolation. Humans concentrate along productive coasts, wetlands, temperate grasslands/plateaus, and mountain valleys; enormous arid and interior regions may have little permanent occupation.

## Evolutionary relevance

The HPGL is also the foundation for later human evolutionary modeling. Geographic isolation, climate, diet, disease, altitude, seasonality, workload, and gene flow can influence regional adaptation. Environment alone does not automatically create distinct human types; selection, time, population size, and isolation must be considered together.

For detailed population zones, movement, barriers, and connectivity, use the standalone **Human Population Geography Layer (HPGL)**.
