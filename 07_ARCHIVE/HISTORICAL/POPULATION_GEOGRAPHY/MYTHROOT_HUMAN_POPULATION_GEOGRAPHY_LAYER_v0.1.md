# MYTHROOT — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY LAYER (HPGL)
## Working Human Geography Framework v0.1

### Purpose

The HPGL determines **where humans can plausibly live, how densely, how they move, and how isolated or connected populations are** before named peoples/cultures are created.

It is a geographic/ecological layer, not a list of cultures.

### Working demographic baseline

Global human population: **~3.5–4 million** (provisional)

Working distribution:
- Hearth: ~1.5 million
- Shattered: ~800,000
- Rift: ~1.0 million
- Lost: ~300,000

These are working numbers, not final demographic canon.

### Residential community model

A typical residential community may contain roughly **80–150 people**, with smaller groups (~40–80) and larger seasonal aggregations (~200–500+) also possible.

This is deliberately different from assuming every human population is permanently settled.

A residential community can be part of a much larger regional network of related communities.

### Mobility model

Human movement may be:

- relatively sedentary around unusually productive resources;
- seasonally mobile;
- semi-nomadic;
- fully nomadic;
- coastal/marine oriented;
- river/lake oriented;
- herd-following;
- plant-season following;
- mixed, changing by season.

Large-animal migrations can pull human populations across large territories. Seasonal plant, fruit, seed, fish, or wetland abundance can do the same.

### Population geography rule

Do **not** distribute humans evenly across a continent.

Population concentrates where several factors overlap:

**food productivity + reliable water + tolerable climate + manageable terrain + safe access + seasonal predictability**

Population becomes sparse where:

**resource scarcity + extreme climate + dangerous terrain + high megafauna pressure + difficult travel**

# HEARTH — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~1.5 million

### General character

Hearth has the strongest geographic connectivity of the four major landmasses.

Its major rivers, grasslands, woodlands, wetlands, and temperate/subtropical climate bands create many viable human corridors.

### Primary population zones

**Major river valleys**
- High food and freshwater reliability.
- Natural movement corridors.
- Strong seasonal aggregation potential.
- High probability of semi-sedentary communities.

**Grassland–woodland boundaries**
- Large herbivore concentrations.
- Broad plant-resource diversity.
- Strong seasonal hunting opportunities.
- Likely support for mobile and semi-mobile communities.

**Wetland/floodplain complexes**
- Aquatic foods, edible plants, game, and reliable water.
- High seasonal productivity.
- Potentially among Hearth's densest human regions.

**Temperate coastal zones**
- Marine resources supplement terrestrial foods.
- Maritime movement can connect otherwise separated communities.

**Highland valleys**
- Lower population density.
- Localized resource concentration.
- Strong geographic isolation where mountain barriers interrupt travel.

### Mobility

Hearth should contain the full spectrum from relatively settled communities to highly mobile groups.

A major recurring pattern is likely to be **herd-following mobility**: people move with large herbivore migrations rather than trying to maintain permanent access to one territory.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Moderate to high overall**, especially along river systems and broad grassland corridors.

Mountain basins and dry interiors create local pockets of reduced exchange.

### Human danger / settlement pressure

Large predators and megafauna discourage isolated camps from occupying every productive patch. Productive areas near water can therefore be surprisingly valuable while still having substantial unoccupied space nearby.

# SHATTERED — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~800,000

### General character

Shattered is defined by fragmentation.

The ocean is not simply a barrier; it is also a resource and transportation system.

### Primary population zones

**Wet western/ocean-influenced islands**
- Higher plant productivity.
- Strong marine productivity.
- Important seasonal refuge during dry periods.
- Likely higher population density than comparable dry islands.

**Seasonally wet interior/island basins**
- Strong pulses of food availability.
- Likely seasonal aggregation.
- Population may disperse during dry periods.

**Coastal productivity zones**
- Fisheries and marine resources.
- Potential permanent or semi-permanent coastal communities.
- Strong inter-island exchange where navigation is practical.

**Dry eastern islands**
- Lower permanent population density.
- Seasonal occupation may remain important.
- Human movement can follow the same resource pulses that drive animal migration.

### Mobility

Shattered strongly favors **seasonal migration**.

The established Farwader ecological pattern demonstrates a plausible cross-water resource cycle: an eastern region can become severely food-limited during the dry season while a wetter western island remains productive.

Humans can respond similarly.

Some groups may:
- migrate inland-to-coast;
- move between islands;
- follow herds;
- follow fisheries;
- follow seasonal plant abundance.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Low to moderate overall**, but highly variable.

Neighboring islands with safe, predictable crossings may exchange people regularly.

Open-ocean distances, dangerous currents, storms, and marine predators can produce strong isolation between other populations.

### Human danger / settlement pressure

Open-water travel is dangerous. Large terrestrial predators can make otherwise productive islands unattractive during certain seasons.

This creates a strong pattern of **seasonally occupied landscapes rather than uniformly occupied landscapes**.

# RIFT — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~1.0 million

### General character

Rift has the most complicated internal geography.

The combination of:
- equatorial climate;
- rift valleys;
- mountains;
- wetlands;
- major lakes/inland seas;
- dry basins;
- volcanic zones

creates many separate human population corridors.

### Primary population zones

**Rift-floor grasslands**
- Large-herbivore resources.
- Broad movement corridors.
- Strong potential for mobile hunting communities.

**Major lake margins**
- Fish and aquatic resources.
- Wetland plants.
- Reliable water.
- Potentially high population density.

**River corridors**
- Connect otherwise isolated regions.
- Provide food, water, transportation, and information exchange.

**Wetlands**
- Extremely productive but seasonally hazardous.
- Likely support both permanent and seasonal communities.

**Highland valleys**
- Strong local resource concentration.
- Reduced population density but potentially stable communities.
- Geographic isolation can produce strong cultural differentiation.

**Dry basins**
- Sparse permanent occupation.
- Seasonal movement tied to water and migrating herds.

### Mobility

Rift should contain many **semi-nomadic and seasonal populations**.

Lake and river peoples may remain relatively localized while hunting populations move over large territories.

A community may alternate between:
- wet-season lowland;
- dry-season upland;
- lake-margin fishing grounds;
- seasonal herd routes.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Moderate overall**, but extremely patchy.

Rivers and lakes create strong corridors.

Mountains, volcanic regions, dry basins, and difficult escarpments create isolated pockets.

### Human danger / settlement pressure

Rift's ecological instability means people cannot assume that a productive location remains productive year-round.

This favors communities with strong environmental knowledge and flexible movement strategies.

# LOST — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~300,000

### General character

Lost has the lowest human population and the strongest potential for long-term geographic isolation.

Humans are present, but enormous areas may contain few or no permanent human settlements.

The origin/history of humans on Lost remains open.

### Primary population zones

**Warm/coastal productive margins**
- Marine productivity.
- Estuaries and coastal plants.
- Stronger human population potential.

**Wetland complexes**
- Reliable water and food.
- Seasonal aggregation.
- Potential refugia during dry periods.

**Temperate grassland/plateau margins**
- Large herbivore biomass.
- Broad hunting territories.
- Strong potential for mobile communities.

**Mountain valleys**
- Localized resources.
- High geographic isolation.
- Potential long-term population pockets.

**Arid basins**
- Extremely low population density.
- Movement tied to water and seasonal food pulses.
- Large stretches may be crossed but rarely occupied.

**Deep interior**
- Very low density.
- Long travel distances.
- Strongest potential for isolated human populations.

### Mobility

Lost strongly favors **high mobility in many regions**.

Nomadic populations may track:
- plateau herbivore migrations;
- wetland cycles;
- fruit/seed seasons;
- water availability;
- coastal marine productivity.

Some coastal and river populations may be comparatively sedentary.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Low overall**, with isolated regional corridors.

Lost is the strongest candidate for:
- long-term population isolation;
- regional physical adaptation;
- major linguistic divergence;
- culturally independent ecological knowledge.

This does **not** automatically imply separate human species. Gene flow remains possible wherever travel corridors exist.

### Human danger / settlement pressure

Lost's independently developed megafauna and flora create the greatest uncertainty for humans.

Some environments may be extremely productive but too dangerous for dense permanent occupation.

# CROSS-CONTINENT HUMAN MOVEMENT

Humans are not isolated to single continents.

Potential long-distance movement pathways include:
- coastal navigation;
- island hopping;
- seasonal sea crossings;
- river systems;
- mountain passes;
- grassland corridors.

However, connectivity should be uneven.

A population may know that another human population exists without maintaining regular contact with it.

### Contact categories

**High contact:** frequent marriage, trade, information exchange, and movement.

**Moderate contact:** seasonal meetings, occasional marriage/trade, shared ecological knowledge.

**Low contact:** rare visitors, indirect information, little gene flow.

**Isolated:** generations may pass without direct contact.

# EVOLUTIONARY / ADAPTIVE IMPLICATIONS

Geography can influence human evolution through:

- climate;
- diet;
- disease environment;
- altitude;
- seasonal stress;
- UV exposure;
- water availability;
- workload and mobility;
- pathogen exposure;
- population isolation.

However:

**environment alone does not automatically produce a new human type.**

The major variable is the combination of **selection + time + population isolation + gene flow**.

Therefore the HPGL identifies likely areas of stronger or weaker divergence without pre-writing physical traits.

---

# PEOPLES CREATION INPUT

The HPGL is intentionally upstream of named peoples.

When a people is eventually created, use:

**Population Zone**
→ **Climate**
→ **Resource Base**
→ **Mobility**
→ **Geographic Barriers**
→ **Neighbor Contact**
→ **Gene Flow**
→ **Ecological Pressures**
→ **Culture / Technology / Social Organization**

Do not create a "mountain people" merely because mountains exist.

Create the people from the actual population history implied by the map.

---

# STATUS

**WORKING CANON / HUMAN GEOGRAPHY SCAFFOLD**

The HPGL establishes plausible population geography and movement patterns.

It does not yet establish:
- named peoples;
- languages;
- religions;
- political systems;
- specific settlements;
- ethnic identities;
- detailed physical adaptations.

Those are downstream layers.
