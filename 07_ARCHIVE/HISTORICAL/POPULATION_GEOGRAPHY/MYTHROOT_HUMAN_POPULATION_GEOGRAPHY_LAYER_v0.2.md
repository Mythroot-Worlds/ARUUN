# MYTHROOT — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY LAYER (HPGL)
## Working Human Geography Framework v0.2

**Status:** UPDATED WORKING CANON / PEOPLE-DEVELOPMENT FOUNDATION

### Purpose

The HPGL determines **where humans can plausibly live, how densely, how they move, and how isolated or connected populations are** before named peoples/cultures are created.

It is a geographic/ecological layer, not a list of cultures.

---

## Working demographic baseline

Global human population: **~3.5–4 million**.

Working distribution:
- Hearth: ~1.5 million
- Shattered: ~800,000
- Rift: ~1.0 million
- Lost: ~300,000

These continental totals are now treated as **locked working demographic canon** for the current Peoples phase, while future fine-grained distributions remain revisable.

### Residential scale

A typical residential community may contain roughly **80–150 people**, with smaller groups (~40–80) and larger seasonal aggregations (~200–500+) also possible.

A residential community is not necessarily a complete cultural population; multiple communities can share language, marriage networks, ecological knowledge, trade, and seasonal gathering sites.

---

## Mobility model

Human movement may be:

- relatively sedentary around unusually productive resources;
- seasonally mobile;
- semi-nomadic;
- fully nomadic;
- coastal/water oriented;
- river/lake oriented;
- herd-following;
- plant-season following;
- mixed, changing by season.

Large-animal migrations and seasonal plant, fruit, seed, fish, or wetland abundance can drive human movement.

### Technology constraint

Aquatic mobility must follow the technological stage actually established for Aruunites.

Early water use can include:
- swimming;
- floating logs/natural flotation;
- simple rafts;
- later simple dugout or comparable craft when woodworking supports them.

**Ships, advanced seafaring, and open-ocean navigation are NOT assumed at this stage.**

---

## Population geography rule

Do **not** distribute humans evenly across a continent.

Population concentrates where several factors overlap:

**food productivity + reliable water + tolerable climate + manageable terrain + safe access + seasonal predictability**

Population becomes sparse where:

**resource scarcity + extreme climate + dangerous terrain + high megafauna pressure + difficult travel**

Population percentage is not the same thing as geographic density. A large population may be dispersed across a huge territory.

# HEARTH — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~1.5 million

### General character

Hearth has the strongest geographic connectivity of the four major landmasses.

Its major rivers, grasslands, woodlands, wetlands, and temperate/subtropical climate bands create many viable human corridors.

### Working distribution

| Zone | Share | Approx. population |
|---|---:|---:|
| River valleys & floodplains | 25% | 375,000 |
| Grassland / woodland mosaic | 30% | 450,000 |
| Wetlands & major lake systems | 15% | 225,000 |
| Temperate/subtropical coasts | 12% | 180,000 |
| Highland valleys | 8% | 120,000 |
| Dry interior / rain shadows | 7% | 105,000 |
| Other scattered/seasonal populations | 3% | 45,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **1,500,000** |

### Primary population zones

**Major river valleys**
- High food and freshwater reliability.
- Natural movement corridors.
- Strong seasonal aggregation potential.
- High probability of semi-sedentary communities.

**Grassland–woodland boundaries**
- Large herbivore concentrations.
- Broad plant-resource diversity.
- Strong seasonal hunting opportunities.
- Likely support for mobile and semi-mobile communities.

**Wetland/floodplain complexes**
- Aquatic foods, edible plants, game, and reliable water.
- High seasonal productivity.
- Potentially among Hearth's densest human regions.

**Temperate coastal zones**
- Marine resources supplement terrestrial foods.
- Water travel may connect nearby communities where technology and conditions allow.

**Highland valleys**
- Lower population density.
- Localized resource concentration.
- Strong geographic isolation where mountain barriers interrupt travel.

### Mobility

Hearth contains the full spectrum from relatively settled communities to highly mobile groups.

A major recurring pattern is **herd-following mobility**: people move with large herbivore migrations rather than trying to maintain permanent access to one territory.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Moderate to high overall**, especially along river systems and broad grassland corridors.

Mountain basins and dry interiors create local pockets of reduced exchange.

### Evolutionary implication

Hearth's high connectivity suppresses extreme regional biological divergence. Climate can create regional gradients, but many pressures are solved culturally through clothing, shelter, fire, seasonal movement, food processing, and ecological knowledge.

# SHATTERED — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~800,000

### General character

Shattered is defined by fragmentation.

The ocean is both a barrier and a resource.

### Working distribution

| Zone | Share | Approx. population |
|---|---:|---:|
| Wet western/ocean-influenced fragments | 30% | 240,000 |
| Coastal productivity zones | 25% | 200,000 |
| Seasonally wet interior basins | 20% | 160,000 |
| Forest/woodland interiors | 10% | 80,000 |
| Dry eastern fragments | 8% | 64,000 |
| Highland/mountain fragments | 5% | 40,000 |
| Small peripheral islands | 2% | 16,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **800,000** |

### Primary population zones

**Wet western/ocean-influenced fragments**
- Higher plant productivity.
- Strong marine productivity.
- Important seasonal refuge during dry periods.

**Seasonally wet interior/island basins**
- Strong pulses of food availability.
- Likely seasonal aggregation.
- Population may disperse during dry periods.

**Coastal productivity zones**
- Fisheries and marine resources.
- Potential permanent or semi-permanent coastal communities.
- Strong inter-fragment exchange where crossings are practical.

**Dry eastern fragments**
- Lower permanent population density.
- Seasonal occupation remains important.
- Movement follows resource pulses.

### Mobility

Shattered strongly favors **seasonal migration**.

Humans may:
- migrate inland-to-coast;
- move between nearby fragments when crossings are practical;
- follow herds;
- follow fisheries;
- follow seasonal plant abundance.

The existence of simple watercraft does not imply advanced ships or open-ocean navigation.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Low to moderate overall**, but highly variable.

Neighboring fragments with safe, predictable crossings may exchange people regularly.

Open-water distances, currents, storms, and marine predators can produce strong isolation between other populations.

### Evolutionary implication

Shattered is the strongest non-Lost environment for population divergence because fragmentation, founder effects, bottlenecks, and variable gene flow can stack with climate.

# RIFT — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~1.0 million

### General character

Rift has the most complicated internal geography.

The combination of equatorial climate, rift valleys, mountains, wetlands, major lakes/inland seas, dry basins, and volcanic zones creates many separate human corridors.

### Working distribution

| Zone | Share | Approx. population |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rift-floor grasslands | 25% | 250,000 |
| Major lake margins | 20% | 200,000 |
| River corridors | 15% | 150,000 |
| Wetlands/floodplains | 15% | 150,000 |
| Highland valleys | 12% | 120,000 |
| Seasonal woodland/uplands | 7% | 70,000 |
| Dry basins/volcanic margins | 6% | 60,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **1,000,000** |

### Primary population zones

**Rift-floor grasslands**
- Large-herbivore resources.
- Broad movement corridors.
- Strong potential for mobile hunting communities.

**Major lake margins**
- Fish and aquatic resources.
- Wetland plants.
- Reliable water.
- Potentially high population density.

**River corridors**
- Connect otherwise isolated regions.
- Provide food, water, transportation, and information exchange.

**Wetlands**
- Extremely productive but seasonally hazardous.
- Likely support both permanent and seasonal communities.

**Highland valleys**
- Strong local resource concentration.
- Reduced population density but potentially stable communities.
- Geographic isolation can produce strong cultural differentiation.

**Dry basins**
- Sparse permanent occupation.
- Seasonal movement tied to water and migrating herds.

### Mobility

Rift contains many **semi-nomadic and seasonal populations**.

A community may alternate between:
- wet-season lowland;
- dry-season upland;
- lake-margin fishing grounds;
- seasonal herd routes.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Moderate overall**, but extremely patchy.

Rivers and lakes create strong corridors.

Mountains, volcanic regions, dry basins, and difficult escarpments create isolated pockets.

### Evolutionary implication

Rift's strongest divergence candidates are highland valleys and other isolated ecological pockets, especially where altitude, cold, rugged terrain, disease, and low gene flow persist together.

# LOST — HUMAN POPULATION GEOGRAPHY

### Working population
~300,000

### General character

Lost has the lowest human population and the strongest potential for long-term geographic isolation.

Humans are present, but enormous areas may contain few or no permanent human settlements.

The **origin/history of humans on Lost remains open**.

Lost's geological isolation is roughly **700 million–1 billion years** as an established deep-time worldbuilding parameter. This is the age of continental/evolutionary isolation, not automatically the duration of Aruunite isolation.

### Working distribution

| Zone | Share | Approx. population |
|---|---:|---:|
| Coastal networks | 38% | 114,000 |
| Grassland / plateau margins | 24% | 72,000 |
| Wetland / lake complexes | 10% | 30,000 |
| Highland valleys | 12% | 36,000 |
| Arid basins | 7% | 21,000 |
| Deep interior | 9% | 27,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **300,000** |

### Primary population zones

**Warm/moist coasts**
- Marine productivity.
- Estuaries and coastal plants.
- Dense biological productivity.

**Cooler/drier current-facing coasts**
- Marine productivity with cooler, drier terrestrial conditions.
- Seasonal grassland and scrub resources.

**Wetland/lake complexes**
- Reliable water and food.
- Seasonal aggregation.
- Potential dry-season refugia.

**Grassland/plateau margins**
- Large herbivore biomass.
- Broad hunting territories.
- Strong potential for mobile communities.

**Mountain valleys**
- Localized resources.
- High geographic isolation.
- Potential long-term population pockets.

**Arid basins**
- Extremely low population density.
- Movement tied to water and seasonal food pulses.

**Deep interior**
- Very low density.
- Long travel distances.
- Strongest potential for isolated human populations.

### Mobility

Lost strongly favors high mobility in many regions.

Nomadic populations may track:
- plateau herbivore migrations;
- wetland cycles;
- fruit/seed seasons;
- water availability;
- coastal productivity.

Some coastal and river populations may be comparatively sedentary.

### Connectivity / gene flow

**Low overall**, with isolated regional corridors.

Lost is the strongest candidate for:
- long-term population isolation;
- regional physical adaptation;
- major linguistic divergence;
- culturally independent ecological knowledge.

This does **not** automatically imply separate human species.

---

# CROSS-CONTINENT HUMAN MOVEMENT

Humans are not assumed to be isolated to single continents.

Potential long-distance movement pathways include:
- coastal navigation;
- island hopping;
- seasonal sea crossings;
- river systems;
- mountain passes;
- grassland corridors.

However, connectivity is uneven.

### Contact categories

**High contact:** frequent marriage, trade, information exchange, and movement.

**Moderate contact:** seasonal meetings, occasional marriage/trade, shared ecological knowledge.

**Low contact:** rare visitors, indirect information, little gene flow.

**Isolated:** generations may pass without direct contact.

---

# EVOLUTIONARY / ADAPTIVE IMPLICATIONS

Geography can influence human evolution through:
- climate;
- diet;
- disease environment;
- altitude;
- seasonal stress;
- UV exposure;
- water availability;
- workload and mobility;
- pathogen exposure;
- population isolation.

However:

> **Environment alone does not automatically produce a new human type.**

The major variable is the combination of **selection + time + population isolation + gene flow**.

Our Aruunite framework also explicitly treats culture and technology as evolutionary buffers:

**environmental pressure → behavior → culture/technology → residual pressure → biological selection**

Cold does not automatically produce a cold-adapted body if clothing, shelter, fire, and seasonal movement solve the problem. Heat does not automatically produce extreme morphology if shade, clothing, water management, and activity timing solve it.

# PEOPLES CREATION INPUT

The HPGL is upstream of named peoples.

When a people is created, use:

**Population Zone**
→ **Climate**
→ **Resource Base**
→ **Mobility**
→ **Geographic Barriers**
→ **Neighbor Contact**
→ **Gene Flow**
→ **Ecological Pressures**
→ **Culture / Technology / Social Organization**
→ **Biological adaptation where residual pressure justifies it**

Do not create a "mountain people" merely because mountains exist.

Create the people from the actual population history implied by the map.
