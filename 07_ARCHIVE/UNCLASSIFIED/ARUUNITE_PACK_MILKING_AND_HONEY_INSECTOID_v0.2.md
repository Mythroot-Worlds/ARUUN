# PART A — PACK / MILK ANIMAL: LOWHORN

## Canonical fauna integration check (§18)

Before inventing a new lineage, the existing herd/browser roster was checked for a viable base:

- **Basingrazer (018)** and **Plateauhorn (019)** — both ruled out: 2.5–3 tonnes, matriarchal megaherds built around outrunning nothing and fighting everything. Too large to feed, house, or restrain with pre-agricultural tools, and their defense strategy (stand and fight with horns/head-shield) doesn't de-escalate around humans the way domestication needs.
- **Thicketback (012)** — considered: right size range (300–400kg), but its defense is scatter-and-freeze in dense cover, and its small family bands don't generalize a stable dominance hierarchy a human could step into. Better fit for a future small-livestock candidate than a pack/milk animal.
- **Ridgehorn (010)** — selected as base lineage. 600–900kg, stocky, short-legged, sure-footed on broken ground, small stable bachelor/matriarchal groups, and a defense posture (horn/skull-plate dominance display) built around contest and hierarchy rather than panic flight. Its highland range also means it's already living in the terrain where pack-carrying is most valuable and where Hearth's existing herd staples (Broadback, Marshtread) are poorly suited.

**Conclusion:** Lowhorn is a **domesticated lowland-adapted regional variant of Ridgehorn**, not a new lineage. This avoids a redundant creature, and domestication plausibly began where wild Ridgehorn populations already range down into foothill terrain bordering Hearth's highland-adjacent human settlements.

## Why domesticate it instead of hunting it

- Ridgehorn is dangerous and slow to bring down cleanly (horn/skull-plate defense), but its dominance-contest behavior means an individual that has already lost a contest, or an orphaned juvenile, will fall in behind a new dominant figure — including, with enough exposure, a human handler. Hunting it is costly and unreliable; managing an already-tolerant animal is not.
- It solves two real Hearth problems at once: moving loads across the same broken highland terrain that makes wheeled transport impossible, and producing a small, dependable milk surplus in a season (dry highland winter) when Broadgrass and Sweetroot are least available.
- It tolerates humans because its wild social structure already tolerates a dominant individual controlling group movement and defending against outside predators — a role a human group can plausibly occupy without the animal needing any new instinct.

## Required Final Profile

**Name:** Lowhorn (working name; domesticated lineage of Ridgehorn, 010)

**Classification:** Existing lineage — domesticated lowland/foothill regional variant of Ridgehorn

**Domestication role:** Dual-purpose (pack + milk) — justified because the same stocky, low-center-of-gravity build that supports a sustained pack load also supports the frame needed to carry a pregnancy and lactation to term without compromising mobility; the two roles don't pull against each other biologically

**Wild range:** Hearth highland-mountain zone and the foothill margin where it grades into open grassland-woodland — Lowhorn specifically develops from populations already living at the lower, warmer edge of that range

**Size:** Domestic form runs smaller than wild Ridgehorn — ~2.1m at the shoulder-equivalent, ~450–600kg, reflecting both selection for manageability and generations of more reliable (if lower-quality) feeding than a wild highland winter provides

**Build:** Stocky quadruped, short thick limbs, wide splayed feet suited to loose scree and packed foothill soil alike, low center of gravity, thickened but reduced horn/skull plating relative to wild Ridgehorn (selected down once fighting other bulls stopped being the main survival pressure); heavy shoulder and neck musculature retained from its climbing ancestry, now repurposed for carrying rather than headbutting

**Locomotion:** Slow, deliberate, sure-footed walker; excels on broken, sloped, or loose terrain where a long-legged grassland animal would stumble; poor sprint speed and no meaningful gallop — this is a load-bearing walker, not a rider's mount

**Diet:** Tough scrub, woody browse, lichen-analogues, and — critically — Coldhusk stubble and Basinsucculent-equivalent lowland forage after harvest; requires no cultivated feed and can be maintained entirely on land too poor or too steep for human food crops, which is exactly why it doesn't compete with Aruunites for their own staple ground

**Social behavior:** Retains small stable group structure with a clear dominance hierarchy; readily accepts a human as the "dominant" figure controlling group movement once habituated young; low panic threshold compared to wild Ridgehorn after generations of selection against the most reactive individuals

**Reproduction:** Sexual maturity around 3–4 years; gestation approximately 10 months; single calf almost always (twins are rare and usually don't both survive); calves stand and follow the herd within a day of birth; humans can and do selectively keep calmer, more tolerant animals for breeding and cull or trade away the most reactive ones — the core mechanism of the domestication pathway below

**Pack capability:** A mature adult sustains roughly 70–90kg of load (co-worker-friendly, under 20% of body mass, reflecting 1.25g's added cost of simply moving its own bulk) across broken highland/foothill terrain for a full day's travel with normal grazing stops; loads above this shorten daily distance sharply rather than causing injury outright — the animal simply slows and refuses to climb steep sections, a built-in safety ceiling rather than a hard failure point

**Milk capability:** Modest but dependable volume — roughly 1.5–2.5 liters per day at peak lactation, well below a purpose-bred dairy animal but meaningful for a small family group; lactation lasts 7–8 months; milk is naturally high-fat (7–9%) and low-volume, an adaptation to feeding a single calf through cold highland/foothill nights rather than a design for surplus; safe harvesting requires leaving the calf first access — Aruunite practice would almost certainly be to milk only after the calf has fed, taking the modest remainder rather than displacing it; richness makes it poor fresh in quantity but well suited to curdling into a dense, storable soft curd, which keeps far longer than the raw milk would on its own

**Secondary products:** Hide (thick, useful for cold-weather covering); horn material (reduced but still present, useful for small tools); dung (dry, burns reasonably well, useful fuel at altitude where wood is scarce); manageable temperament also gives it real value as a predator-warning animal, since its group-vigilance behavior carries over from the wild form

**Domestication pathway:** Wild Ridgehorn populations at the foothill margin already tolerate distant human presence better than deep-highland populations do, simply from more frequent incidental contact → orphaned or defeated-contest juveniles habituate quickly to a human "dominant" figure if handled young → humans begin deliberately keeping and protecting the calmest juveniles rather than only hunting adults → calmer animals are bred forward, more reactive ones culled or traded away → within several generations a recognizably tamer, smaller, more human-tolerant population exists alongside the wild foothill form

**Ecological consequences:** Grazing pressure on foothill scrub increases where domestic herds are kept, potentially pushing wild Ridgehorn populations to range higher into less accessible terrain to avoid competition; escaped or traded domestic animals could re-establish in foothill range and interbreed with wild populations, gradually blurring the wild/domestic boundary; dung deposition around camps changes local soil and plant composition over time; because Lowhorn eats forage humans can't, direct food competition with people stays low

**Human cultural significance:** A dependable dry-season milk source and a way to move goods across terrain no human back or unaided sled easily manages — plausibly one of the more prestige-bearing possessions a highland-adjacent Aruunite family could have, given the generational investment required to build a calm breeding line

**Regional limitations:** Only viable where wild Ridgehorn range naturally extends — Hearth's highland-foothill zone. Groups on Rift, Shattered, or Lost have no wild population to draw from at all; even within Hearth, lowland grassland/floodplain groups have less reason to adopt it since Broadback and Marshtread already cover their transport-adjacent needs and Lowhorn's climbing advantage is wasted on flat ground

**Evolutionary rationale:** Ridgehorn's original adaptation — stocky, low-slung, sure-footed, defense through contest rather than flight — already solves the two hardest problems in domesticating a large 1.25g animal (skeleton that can bear real load, temperament that can be managed) as an accident of solving a highland survival problem millions of years before any human existed

**Major tradeoff:** In exchange for a load-bearing, milk-producing animal, Aruunites give up flexibility — Lowhorn is slow, can't be pushed fast under load, refuses genuinely steep or loose ground with a full pack, and its milk yield never approaches what a purpose-selected dairy animal on rich, cultivated feed could produce. It is a durable generalist, not a specialist at either job.

**Writer-facing description:** Lowhorn plants each broad foot like it's testing the ground's opinion first, and on the switchback trails above a foothill camp that patience is worth more than speed ever would be. A loaded string of them moving single-file up a scree slope looks unhurried right up until you try to keep pace on foot; by evening, when the pack frames come off and the calf gets first turn at the udder, the animal that spent all day refusing to be rushed stands perfectly still for milking, like the whole day's stubbornness was only ever about the trail.

# PART B — HONEY-INSECTOID: WAXDRIFTER

## Quality-test framing, addressed up front

- **Could it exist without humans?** Yes — it is Goldbloom's primary pollinator, and its colony structure and stored food exist entirely independent of any human interest in raiding it.
- **Does the stored food make sense as an evolved colony resource?** Yes — see below; it's a starvation buffer for a long-blooming but seasonally staggered nectar source, not a substance invented to be sweet.
- **Does Goldbloom benefit?** Yes — Waxdrifter is Goldbloom's primary and most effective pollinator; the tree's unusually long, staggered bloom period exists specifically to keep Waxdrifter colonies fed across the season, and the two are mutually dependent.
- **Would humans realistically risk harvesting it?** Yes, and it costs them something to try — see Defense.
- **Can harvesting happen without destroying the colony?** Yes, with real limits — see Human harvest.
- **Does 1.25g meaningfully affect it?** Yes — see Locomotion; this is not a scaled-up Earth bee.
- **More interesting than "Aruunite bee"?** The colony's non-eusocial, matriline-helper social structure and the paste-form (not liquid) stored sweetener are both direct departures from the Earth-bee template.

## Required Final Profile

**Name:** Waxdrifter (working name)

**Classification:** New lineage — no existing canonical insectoid fills this role; justified per the Fauna Function Matrix as the missing pollinator half of the already-canonical Goldbloom relationship

**Ecological role:** Primary pollinator and nectar collector for Goldbloom; secondary minor pollinator for a handful of other Hearth woodland-margin flowering plants that bloom in Goldbloom's shadow; larvae also consume a small amount of stored paste and pollen mass, making the colony's food store partly a nursery resource rather than purely a human-facing prize

**Size:** Body roughly 4–5cm long, notably larger-bodied than an Earth honeybee but with a proportionally larger wing area — mass around 2–3 grams

**Anatomy:** Broad, stiffened forewings that do most of the lift work, paired with smaller rear wings used mainly for steering and braking rather than power — a split-function wing pair rather than Earth-insect wing symmetry; a short, dense-bristled proboscis-analogue suited to Goldbloom's flower shape; no stinger — its defense is chemical rather than piercing (see Defense); dense, short body hair that both traps pollen and reduces heat loss during the cool highland-margin mornings when Goldbloom often blooms

**Locomotion:** Flight is short-range and energetically expensive under 1.25g — Waxdrifter does not sustain long cruising flight the way an Earth bee does. Instead it flies in short, controlled bursts between adjacent blooms and spends much of its foraging time climbing along branches and bark between flights, using flight only to cross gaps climbing can't cover. Its oversized forewings exist specifically to make each short burst cheap enough to be worth taking; a smaller-winged version of this insectoid simply couldn't afford to fly at all under Aruun's gravity. Maneuverability in the air is poor — it does not hover or dart — but climbing agility is excellent

**Diet:** Nectar (primary, from Goldbloom and secondary flowering plants), pollen (protein source, especially for larvae), no predation

**Social structure:** Communal matriline colony — not strictly eusocial. A colony is founded and dominated by a small handful of reproductive females (typically 2–4, often related) rather than a single queen; the bulk of the colony are non-breeding foragers and nest-defenders, but unlike Earth eusocial insects these individuals retain functional reproductive capacity and can begin laying if the founding matriline dies or is lost, allowing a colony to recover from a founder's death rather than collapsing outright. This is the "evolutionarily different" solution the design brief asked for in place of an assumed queen/worker/drone system

**Nest:** Built in hollow sections of mature Goldbloom trunks and large deadwood nearby, rarely underground; internal structure is a cluster of horizontal paste-storage cells built from a chewed-wood-and-wax paste rather than pure wax comb, giving the structure more insulation and structural rigidity than a thin wax comb would provide; colony size runs small by Earth-hive standards — a few hundred individuals at most, reflecting the higher individual energy cost of flight limiting how large a flying-forager workforce can be sustained

**Reproduction:** Reproductive females mate once per season with visiting males from other colonies (males do not join or defend a nest, and die shortly after mating); each reproductive female lays a modest number of eggs across the season rather than a continuous Earth-bee-style laying rate, consistent with a colony that isn't trying to replace a large number of short-lived flight workers

**Stored food:** Waxdrifter does not produce a liquid honey. Collected nectar is mixed with a waxy salivary secretion and repeatedly worked by foragers until it sets into a dense, non-flowing paste — thicker than honey, closer to a firm preserve — stored in sealed cells. This solves the same problem Earth honey solves (a stable, low-spoilage sugar reserve to survive gaps between Goldbloom's staggered bloom pulses) but the paste form is specifically more stable and less prone to fermenting or leaking in storage than a liquid would be, which matters more for a colony that can't constantly patrol and repair a large comb structure given how metabolically expensive flight is for individual foragers. It is safe for humans to eat, either directly or dissolved in water; deliberately watering it down and letting it sit is a plausible, low-effort route to an early fermented beverage

**Human harvest:** A mature colony keeps a working surplus beyond what it needs to survive a bloom gap; that surplus, concentrated in the outer/newer storage cells, can be removed without touching the older, deeper cells the colony actually depends on — meaning a colony can be harvested from repeatedly across a season if a person takes only the outer surplus and leaves the core untouched. Destroying the whole nest for a one-time haul is easier but ends the relationship; leaving it standing rewards a return visit next season, which is the likely origin of "managed wild colony" behavior — people learning which hollow trunks host productive colonies and returning to them rather than raiding indiscriminately

**Defense:** No stinger; disturbed defenders spray a bitter, mildly caustic chemical secretion from paired abdominal glands, causing skin and eye irritation rather than a piercing wound — unpleasant enough to reliably drive off a casual disturbance, rarely dangerous on its own unless someone is sprayed repeatedly at close range or in the eyes. Colony defense radius is short — defenders rarely pursue more than a few meters from the nest entrance, since flight is too costly to spend chasing. Smoke reliably suppresses the spray response (the secretion glands appear to seize/vent when the colony's ambient air is disrupted), giving Aruunites an learnable, low-technology way to reduce the cost of harvesting

**Plant relationships:** Goldbloom's unusually long, staggered bloom period exists specifically to keep Waxdrifter colonies fed continuously enough to survive on short-range flight foraging alone; in turn, Waxdrifter is responsible for the bulk of Goldbloom's pollination — the two are close to obligate mutualists. Secondary pollination benefits extend to a handful of other Hearth woodland-margin flowering plants within Waxdrifter's short foraging range of a Goldbloom grove

**Ecological importance:** If Waxdrifter disappeared, Goldbloom's reproduction would drop sharply (no comparably effective alternative pollinator is established in the current fauna library), threatening the entire honey-analogue system, the woodland-margin plants that benefit from secondary pollination, and any human population that has come to depend on managed colonies

**Human cultural significance:** Beyond the paste itself, colony locations plausibly become known, named, remembered places — a productive hollow trunk tended across generations is a real, transmissible piece of family or group knowledge, not a resource anyone can simply stumble into and exploit successfully

**Regional limitations:** Confined to Hearth's woodland-margin zone, tracking Goldbloom's own range exactly; Aruunite populations on Rift, Shattered, or Lost, or even deep-interior/highland Hearth groups away from Goldbloom groves, have no access to it at all and would need an entirely different (currently undeveloped) sweetener source

**Evolutionary rationale:** A high-gravity world makes sustained flight expensive enough that an insectoid pollinator needs either a very small body (limiting foraging range severely) or an oversized-wing, short-burst-flight compromise like Waxdrifter's — paired with a stored-food strategy (dense paste over thin liquid comb) that reduces how often costly flight-based nest maintenance and defense is required

**Major tradeoff:** Humans risk a real, if rarely dangerous, chemical-spray injury to get at the paste, and can only take a sustainable surplus without ending future harvests — the plant-insect system rewards patience and repeat visits over a single maximal raid

**Writer-facing description:** A Goldbloom grove in bloom sounds wrong before it sounds anything else — no droning hum overhead, just a constant papery rustle of wings taking short, deliberate hops from flower to flower along the same branch. Anyone foolish enough to shake that trunk learns fast why the old smoke-and-approach-slow method got taught to them in the first place, and anyone patient enough to leave the deep cells alone gets to learn it again next season.

## Review status

Both Lowhorn and Waxdrifter remain **PROPOSED WORKING FAUNA**.

### Current interpretation locks for the Peoples phase

- **Lowhorn:** managed mobile animal, not intensive livestock; small traveling groups may keep working animals, breeding animals, and a few reserves. Milk is a secondary benefit to transport utility. Animals may be eaten when old, injured, surplus, or otherwise no longer worth maintaining, and replacements may be acquired through local breeding, exchange, or capture/training of suitable wild individuals.
- **Waxdrifter:** managed wild colony relationship, not conventional beekeeping. Aruunites identify productive colonies, protect or avoid destroying them, harvest surplus stored paste, and return seasonally when routes permit.
- Neither candidate is yet canonical.
- Small/background fauna remain intentionally open and should be handled later at ecological-family/archetype level unless a species becomes important to a people or story.
